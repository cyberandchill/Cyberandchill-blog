How to Know Whether Identity Work Would Actually Suit You

Before committing months to IAM, try the kinds of problems the work involves.

A salary goal or someone else's career story can attract your attention. It can't tell you whether you enjoy access reasoning, sign-in investigations, or coordinating across system owners.

A few small exercises can provide better evidence of fit. You don't need to buy a large course to discover which parts interest you enough to keep learning—or which responsibilities you'd prefer to avoid.

Test your interest with an access-design exercise

Imagine a fictional project with contributors, reviewers, and an owner.

Without opening a tool, decide:

  • What actions should each role be allowed to perform?
  • Who approves assignment to each role?
  • What changes when a contributor becomes a reviewer?
  • What happens when a person leaves the project?
  • How should a temporary exception be handled?

Notice whether these questions hold your attention. Do you want to clarify the boundaries and test the result, or do you mainly like the career label attached to the exercise?

You don't need to enjoy every administrative detail. This is a small way to examine the central problem of appropriate access.

Treat the result as one data point. Combine it with a technical exercise and real job descriptions before deciding that an entire specialty does or doesn't suit you.

Investigate a controlled failure

Use a safe test environment and a narrow scenario you understand.

Establish a working behavior, introduce one reversible change, and inspect the evidence when the result differs. Keep the experiment isolated from real users and production systems.

Notice whether you enjoy narrowing the cause or only enjoy the moment when the error disappears.

Identity work can require patient investigation across several boundaries. A small lab will not reproduce the whole job, but it can reveal whether that style of reasoning interests you.

Explain the result to someone without the same background

Describe the user's problem, the relevant access decision, and what you verified.

Avoid relying on acronyms to carry the explanation. If the listener cannot tell why the change matters, revise the story rather than adding more terminology.

Communication is part of many identity roles because the work connects technical systems with business responsibilities.

You may enjoy the investigation but dislike stakeholder coordination. That is useful information when choosing among support, engineering, governance, consulting, or application-focused roles.

Read the workday behind several IAM lanes

Compare actual role descriptions for different specialties. Some emphasize administration and operational requests. Others focus on integrations, governance processes, privileged access, or customer-facing applications. A single example does not represent the entire field.

Use the main IAM specialties to organize the comparison, then inspect the responsibilities and conditions in roles you would consider.

Ask which tasks you would want more of and which you would tolerate only in limited amounts. The answer can help you choose a lane rather than reject or idealize all of IAM at once.

Check the working conditions separately from the subject

You might enjoy identity work and still dislike a particular role's schedule, travel, coverage, or management style.

Do not confuse a bad-fit environment with proof that the technical specialty is wrong for you. Equally, do not assume every identity role offers remote freedom or a quiet workday.

Ask concrete questions about ordinary work, support, and expectations.

Career fit includes both the problems you solve and the conditions under which you solve them. Evaluate both before making a decision based on someone else's lifestyle story.

Make a small commitment based on the evidence

After the exercises, choose a next step: a focused lab, a deeper conversation, a targeted application, or a different specialty to investigate.

You are allowed to learn that a direction is not appealing. That is a better outcome than spending a year following a roadmap because the destination sounded impressive.

If the work does interest you, use that interest to support a concrete plan rather than an indefinite collection of content.
