You Can Have Transferable Experience and Still Have Real Skill Gaps

Relevant IT experience and real IAM skill gaps can exist at the same time.

Map the experience to specific responsibilities instead of asking whether your entire background “counts.” Account and access work may transfer without establishing that you've configured integrations or investigated synchronization.

A clear map lets you acknowledge the strengths and the missing capability. You can apply where the fit is credible while building evidence for the part you haven't yet demonstrated.

Label the kind of evidence behind each requirement

Take one responsibility from a suitable posting and classify your experience.

Category What it means
Performed at work You can explain your real contribution and scope.
Assisted or observed You participated without owning the full task.
Completed in a lab You demonstrated it in a controlled practice environment.
Studied only You understand material but haven't applied it.
Unfamiliar You need to investigate the requirement.

Write a short example beside the category rather than assigning yourself credit from a product name alone.

This prevents both exaggeration and unnecessary self-dismissal. One platform feature isn't the whole platform, but a general support title doesn't erase relevant work.

If you can't describe the example, investigate the claim further before using it in an application.

Give transferable experience a specific destination

“Good at troubleshooting” is broad. Explain what transfers.

A network-support background may help you separate reachability problems from application behavior. Service-desk work may provide experience verifying requests, communicating with users, and following controlled access procedures.

That does not automatically prove expertise in every authentication protocol or access policy.

Connect the transferable skill to a responsibility in the target role, then identify the additional knowledge needed. This creates a learning plan instead of an argument about whether your previous job was technical enough.

Choose a gap that changes your ability to compete

Not every missing item deserves equal attention. Suppose the role emphasizes application sign-in troubleshooting and lists several optional technologies. You already handle account issues but have never connected a test application to an identity provider.

A small integration project may address a more important gap than studying every optional product name.

Define what you need to demonstrate: successful sign-in, one controlled failure, evidence used in the investigation, and the limits of the test.

The job-posting clues that help you choose a useful lab can help you make that decision.

Describe the gap without talking yourself out of applying

Honesty does not require presenting your background as inadequate.

You can say: “My professional experience includes account administration and access troubleshooting. I haven't owned production SSO integrations, so I built a test integration to understand the flow and practice diagnosing a configuration error.”

That statement gives the employer three useful facts: what you've done at work, what you haven't done, and what you've done to learn.

Avoid replacing those facts with either “I'm basically already an engineer” or “I don't have any relevant experience.” Both hide the actual situation.

Let the employer evaluate the remaining distance

Some roles require immediate independent ownership. Others can support someone with a strong foundation and a bounded learning gap.

You won't discover which situation applies by privately comparing yourself with an imaginary perfect candidate.

Apply when the responsibilities and requirements are plausibly within reach, while respecting genuine prerequisites and your practical constraints. Ask how the team supports new hires and what they expect someone to handle independently at the start.

That conversation provides information your study plan cannot generate by itself.

Update the map as evidence improves

After a lab, interview, or approved work project, revise the relevant category. Don't upgrade a whole skill area because one exercise went well.

Record the specific task you can now explain and demonstrate. Keep the next uncertainty visible.

Over time, this produces a more accurate view of your readiness than counting years or certifications alone.
