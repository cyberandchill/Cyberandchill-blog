The Difference Between Being Valuable to Your Boss and Valuable to the Market

Your current team knows why you're valuable. A future employer has to understand it from the evidence you provide.

Translate local knowledge into recognizable responsibilities and capabilities. Another employer may not know your internal systems, unusual processes, or how often colleagues rely on you.

That doesn't make the experience worthless. It means you need to explain the underlying work clearly—and notice when the current role is building dependency without developing broader capability.

Internal value includes local knowledge

You may know the undocumented exception, the right application owner, or the reason an old process works the way it does.

That knowledge can be genuinely valuable to the organization. It helps work continue and prevents repeated mistakes.

But another employer cannot necessarily use the same details. They need to understand the underlying capability: investigation, coordination, access administration, or process improvement.

The task is to translate the value, not dismiss it because it is tied to one environment.

Market evidence needs a recognizable responsibility

A future employer needs to know what you personally did and why it is relevant to their work.

“Everyone came to me” is difficult to evaluate. “Investigated recurring sign-in failures and prepared evidence for the application and identity teams” describes a more portable contribution when it is true.

Keep the scope accurate. Don't replace local detail with an inflated generic title.

The strongest translation identifies the problem, your judgment, and the verified outcome without exposing confidential information.

Check whether local expertise also develops transferable ability

Being the only person who remembers an undocumented exception can matter greatly inside a company. Ask what learning accompanies that dependency.

Use three checks:

  1. Principle: Can you explain why the process works, beyond the local steps?
  2. Method: Have you developed a tool, investigation approach, or decision skill another team would recognize?
  3. Demonstration: Could you show similar reasoning safely in a fictional environment?

The answers may reveal strong transferable capability. If they don't, choose a focused development objective rather than assuming more years will automatically expand your options.

This isn't a criticism of loyalty, stable work, or valuable local knowledge. It is a way to examine whether the next year of experience will give you something additional to explain when you eventually consider another opportunity.

Use job descriptions to test the translation

Compare suitable roles with your generalized examples. Look for the same responsibility expressed in different terms. A job may call for lifecycle administration while your resume says onboarding and access changes. The connection may be real, but you need to explain it.

Also identify the genuine gaps. Familiarity with one internal process doesn't establish that you can configure a different platform independently.

Use an experience-to-requirement inventory to separate transferable evidence from missing capability.

Build public proof without taking private work with you

A fictional lab can demonstrate the principle behind a professional task.

For example, you might recreate an approval-to-access workflow with invented users and resources. Keep it clearly labeled as a lab, and describe your professional responsibility separately.

Do not copy internal documents, logs, or architecture into a personal repository to make the case more convincing.

The purpose is to show your understanding while respecting the boundaries of the work that helped you develop it.

Use both kinds of value in your career decision

You may have a strong internal opportunity that rewards your contribution and expands your responsibility. You may also find that the organization values keeping you in the current role more than developing the next one.

Discuss the path explicitly and compare it with realistic external options.

The decision doesn't have to be emotional or adversarial. You are evaluating where your capability, priorities, and opportunity fit best.
