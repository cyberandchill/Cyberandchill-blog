The Problem With Following Someone Else's Certification Roadmap

Someone else's certification roadmap shows one possible sequence. It doesn't establish which step makes sense from your starting point.

Their prior experience, target job, budget, employer requirements, and study time may differ from yours. The same exam can serve a different purpose before or after several years of relevant work.

Use the roadmap to identify decisions worth investigating. Then adapt the sequence around your actual gaps rather than treating another person's career as instructions.

Compare the experience that came before each credential

Before copying a roadmap, examine what the person already knew.

Ask:

  • Had they worked in support, networking, systems, or development?
  • Which relevant responsibilities could they already perform?
  • Was the credential introducing a subject or documenting existing knowledge?
  • Do you already understand material that was new to them?

A certification earned after years of related work doesn't represent the same career starting point as an exam pursued before practical exposure.

Write down the responsibilities you can explain now. Include both strengths and limitations. That gives you a basis for deciding which parts of the sequence transfer.

Repeating the entire path may feel safer, but it can consume time without addressing your main gap. Your plan should start with your evidence, not the first badge in someone else's biography.

Identify the job the roadmap was designed to reach

A path toward security management, cloud engineering, or a particular vendor role may not fit an IAM operations target.

Look at the responsibilities rather than the broad label “cybersecurity.”

If the destination differs, the sequence may still contain useful concepts, but its order and priorities need review.

Don't assume that a respected credential is the best next move simply because it appears near the top of somebody else's successful story.

Check the conditions that made the sequence practical

The person may have had employer funding, access to a practice environment, an internal promotion opportunity, or a requirement tied to a particular job.

You don't need to know every private detail. You do need to recognize that those conditions can affect the decision.

Verify current exam objectives, prerequisites, costs, and renewal rules through official sources before committing.

A roadmap from an earlier period may also refer to products or requirements that have changed.

Replace the sequence with a gap map

List the responsibilities in several suitable roles and compare them with your evidence.

Then identify what would address each gap: study, a certification, a lab, an approved work project, or clearer positioning.

This may produce a shorter plan than the roadmap. It may also reveal a foundation you genuinely need before attempting the specialty.

The goal is not to skip difficulty. It is to spend effort on the capability and signals your next move actually requires.

Write a replacement sequence in your own words: target responsibility, evidence already available, missing capability, and next activity. This makes each step accountable to your situation instead of making the entire plan depend on someone else having chosen wisely.

Keep useful principles even when you change the steps

A roadmap may model consistent study, practical application, or deliberate specialization. Those principles can remain useful even when the specific certifications don't fit.

Separate the method from the exact purchases. Consider why copying an exact certification path can mislead when a personal success story starts to feel like a universal instruction manual.

You can learn from the person's decisions without assuming their chronology is your best route.

Review the adapted plan against results

After a project, application, or technical conversation, ask whether the selected learning addressed the gap you intended.

If the evidence points elsewhere, adjust the plan. Don't keep completing steps solely because they were printed in a graphic or recommended by someone with an impressive job.

Your plan should become more specific as you learn about the work and your own capability.
