The Day I Realized Being Busy in IT Wasn't the Same as Moving Up

Being busy in IT can show that your team needs you without showing that you're getting closer to the next role you want.

I pay more attention to what the work makes me capable of doing than how full the week feels. That distinction helped me make sense of my path through network support, security software, and identity.

The useful question is what changed: responsibility, understanding, evidence, or career options—not only the number of tasks completed.

A busy week can still repeat the same lesson

There is real value in reliable operational work. Users need help, systems need attention, and somebody has to handle the routine.

But repetition and progression are not identical. If every week gives you more of the same narrow task, you may become faster without becoming better qualified for a different responsibility. That can be fine for a period, but it should not be confused with an automatic career ladder.

Ask what you can explain or handle now that you couldn't six months ago.

The next role may require a different kind of evidence

A strong support record does not always show that you can design an integration, investigate a broader failure, or own an access process.

Those responsibilities may require additional learning or an opportunity to practice. You need to identify the gap before assuming another year will close it.

This is where a real job description becomes useful.

Read the work the employer wants completed. Then compare it with your examples, not just your tenure. The comparison may reveal a smaller and more manageable next step than the broad phrase “move into cybersecurity.”

I would measure progress by responsibility and options

Useful progress might mean taking on a more complete investigation, understanding a system boundary, or becoming able to explain a problem to another team.

It might also mean recognizing that a role isn't the direction you want.

Those outcomes don't always produce an immediate title change. They still give you better information and stronger options.

A certificate can be part of that progress when it supports the target. It shouldn't become the only visible measure while applications, practical work, and career decisions remain untouched.

Make one part of the week serve the next move

You don't need to turn every evening into another work shift.

Choose a small recurring block for a defined career action: compare suitable roles, build a focused lab, document a relevant example, or submit a targeted application.

The important part is that the action has an output.

“Study identity” is too broad. “Demonstrate a fictional employee's old access being removed after a team transfer” is something you can finish, explain, and use to evaluate your understanding.

Keep the current job separate from the portfolio

Don't use employer access as permission to experiment or publish internal material.

Learn through authorized responsibilities, and build public examples with fictional data in an environment you control. Describe professional experience without exposing private systems or taking credit for other people's work.

That boundary protects both the employer and the credibility of your career story.

Use your current work as a source of learning questions, not as a collection of material to copy into a public repository.

Review the month for capability and options, not just effort

At the end of the month, check for concrete progress:

  • Direction: Do you understand the next role more clearly?
  • Evidence: Can you explain a stronger example of your work?
  • Capability: Can you demonstrate something you couldn't before?
  • Options: Have you learned more about opportunities you would accept?

A demanding month may leave little room for additional projects. Acknowledge that and narrow the next objective rather than treating every setback as a need to work more hours.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook puts this into a $9 guide: choose an IAM direction, use relevant experience, build proof, and take a concrete next step.

Doing your current job well matters. Give at least part of your development effort a clear connection to the work you want next.
