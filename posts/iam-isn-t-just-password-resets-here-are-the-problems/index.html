IAM Isn't Just Password Resets. Here Are the Problems Specialists Own.

Password resets are one visible identity task. Specialists may also integrate applications, manage access changes, organize reviews, control administrative permissions, or investigate cross-system failures.

Those are different career directions, not a checklist every IAM professional must already master.

Use the problems to understand the field. Then choose a direction connected to your current experience and interests instead of trying to learn every product carrying an identity label.

1. Connecting an application to the sign-in system

A company wants employees to use an approved sign-in method for a business application.

Someone has to understand the application's integration requirements, configure the relevant components, test the flow, and coordinate responsibility when it fails. The work may also involve separate questions about user provisioning and application roles.

The specialist problem is not simply “turn on SSO.” It is making the intended access path work and explaining where the boundaries are.

A useful learning project demonstrates the flow and one controlled failure in a test application.

2. Making access follow the employee lifecycle

A person joins, changes teams, and eventually leaves.

At each stage, the organization needs access to match the current approved responsibilities. That means more than creating an account on the first day and remembering to disable it on the last.

The problem includes authoritative information, approvals, implementation, exceptions, and verification.

A fictional joiner-mover-leaver scenario can demonstrate how access should change over time. The transfer is especially useful because it requires deciding what to remove as well as what to add.

3. Deciding who should still have existing access

An access review asks whether a current entitlement remains appropriate.

The reviewer needs enough context to make a decision: the identity, the resource, the access level, the business reason, and the responsible owner. A completed review also needs a way to carry out the decision.

Microsoft's access-review overview illustrates one platform implementation of this governance function.

For learning, you can begin with synthetic data and a decision process before adding an enterprise tool. The important skill is understanding the decision and follow-through.

4. Controlling powerful administrative permissions

Some actions can change systems or affect many other users. They deserve a deliberate approach to who can perform them, under what conditions, and with what accountability.

This is where privileged-access work becomes relevant. The exact controls depend on the environment. A specialist may investigate elevation, scope, approval, session accountability, or other aspects of administrative access.

You can start by comparing an ordinary user's task with a privileged maintenance task and explaining why the authority required should differ. Avoid treating a broad administrator role as the default solution.

5. Investigating a failure across several owners

A user reports that access is broken, but the relevant path crosses a directory, identity provider, application, and perhaps other systems.

Someone needs to establish which stage failed and coordinate the next action with the right owner.

That requires technical reasoning and communication. “The password is correct” or “the identity side looks fine” may not answer the user's actual problem.

Use a login flow with several possible failure points to practice distinguishing evidence from assumptions and keeping the investigation tied to one attempt.

Choose the problem that gives your learning a purpose

Read several suitable job descriptions and compare four things:

  1. Repeated work: Which problems appear across the roles?
  2. Existing evidence: Which connect to responsibilities you've already performed?
  3. Manageable gap: What could a focused project help you demonstrate?
  4. Work preference: Which investigations or decisions would you want to repeat?

This is more practical than memorizing every identity acronym before applying anywhere.
