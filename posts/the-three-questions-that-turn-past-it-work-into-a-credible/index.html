The Three Questions That Turn Past IT Work Into a Credible Career Pivot

To explain how past IT work supports an IAM move, answer three questions: what were you responsible for, what judgment did you use, and how did you verify completion?

Those answers make the real work easier to see without making the task larger than it was.

Use examples you can discuss honestly and within confidentiality limits. Then connect the responsibility to the identity role you're pursuing, keeping professional experience, independent practice, and study clearly distinguished.

Question one: What was your actual responsibility?

Start with the request or problem and the boundary around your role.

“Managed accounts” could mean executing approved changes, investigating account failures, maintaining the process, or designing the access model. Those are different responsibilities.

For an illustrative example, suppose you added users to approved project groups after checking the request. Your responsibility was to carry out the authorized change accurately, not to decide independently who deserved project access.

That boundary is useful information. It shows that you understand the difference between business approval and technical execution.

Describe the checks and decisions you genuinely made

Look for judgment within the task, not drama to add to the story.

For an access request, relevant checks might include:

  • Confirming the intended identity.
  • Checking that the requested resource was specified correctly.
  • Noticing that a requested group didn't match the stated need.
  • Recognizing that approval was missing.
  • Knowing when the request required escalation rather than completion.

Only use examples that reflect your actual responsibility.

A straightforward procedure can still require care. Explain what you checked and which conditions would have stopped you from proceeding.

If the process was fixed, say so. Following it accurately is different from designing it, but that doesn't make the experience worthless. The goal is to show that you understood and exercised the judgment your role required, not to manufacture a senior-level decision you never made.

Question three: What evidence showed completion?

A changed setting is not always the same as a verified outcome.

What did you check after the action? Did you confirm the membership, test the relevant behavior within your authorization, obtain confirmation from the responsible party, or document the handoff?

State the scope accurately. If you verified the directory change but did not test the application, say so.

This question often reveals the difference between describing activity and describing a responsibility with a clear finish line.

Combine the answers into a short example

A supported explanation might follow this structure: “I processed approved project-access requests. I checked the identity and requested group, escalated mismatches, made the authorized change, and recorded the verification required by our process.”

Use only the parts that match your experience.

You can then explain why the example is relevant to identity administration: it involved account accuracy, access boundaries, controlled changes, and evidence of completion.

The wording does not require a new job title or a claim that you designed the whole IAM system.

Use a lab to explore the part you did not own

Perhaps the workplace task left you curious about how the group affected a resource or how removal changed access.

You can investigate that independently with fictional users and your own test environment. Keep the lab separate from the professional example.

The method for rewriting service desk experience without exaggeration helps preserve that distinction across a resume and interview answer.

The combination can be strong: real experience executing a responsibility, plus deliberate practice to understand the wider system.

Build a small inventory instead of one polished story

Apply the three questions to several relevant tasks: onboarding, access troubleshooting, MFA support, group changes, or offboarding work.

You will see which examples are strongest and where your understanding is thin. That is useful preparation for both role selection and technical study.

Do not publish internal records to prove the stories. Keep employer information private and describe your contribution within the disclosure rules that apply.
