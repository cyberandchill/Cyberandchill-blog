Is Your IT Salary Stuck Because You're Learning the Wrong Things?

A stalled salary doesn't prove you're studying the wrong subject.

Check whether the obstacle is skill depth, visibility, applications, or the current employer's limits. Each calls for a different response.

Buying another course before diagnosing the problem can make the week busier without improving your options. Compare suitable roles with your actual evidence, then run one targeted change so you can learn whether it addresses the obstacle.

Check whether your learning matches a real role

Look at your last few months of study. What job responsibilities did that work prepare you to perform?

A list of courses, tools, and exam topics is not an answer by itself. You need to connect the learning to tasks an employer actually expects someone to handle.

For example, studying several security products may be less relevant to an identity administrator application than demonstrating controlled account changes and sign-in troubleshooting.

Save a small set of realistic job postings and compare responsibilities. Don't select only unusually high compensation advertisements and assume they represent your likely outcome.

Check whether you need deeper practice, not a new subject

You may already be studying the right area but stopping at familiarity.

Test your understanding:

  1. Can you explain why the configuration works?
  2. Can you predict what changes when a relevant condition changes?
  3. Can you investigate a failure without replaying the tutorial from the beginning?
  4. Can you support the explanation with observed evidence?

Weak answers may point toward a deeper test of the existing project rather than another technology or certification.

Choose one responsibility and define what would demonstrate it. That turns “I've studied IAM” into a bounded claim such as understanding a particular access decision or investigating a tested sign-in failure.

You still need to consider the other career obstacles. Deeper practice helps when capability is the problem; it won't create an internal vacancy or submit applications you've avoided making.

Check whether people can see the capability you already have

A strong background can be hidden behind broad resume language.

“Supported users” does not explain whether you handled access approvals, investigated authentication issues, or documented a complex recovery. Review your actual work and make the relevant responsibility visible without exaggerating it.

The same applies to a portfolio. A link to a large repository doesn't help much when the first screen doesn't explain the problem and result.

The relationship between a clear resume and a useful GitHub link is worth fixing before assuming you need more technical training.

Check whether the limitation is the current employer

Your organization may not have an opening, a suitable pay structure, or work that develops the specialty you want. More study may improve your options, but it won't create an internal position automatically.

Ask concrete questions about advancement: what responsibilities would change, what evidence is required, who makes the decision, and when it could be reviewed.

Compare those answers with external opportunities that fit your constraints.

You don't have to leave immediately to learn that the current role has a limited path. You do need to distinguish a real development plan from repeated encouragement with no decision attached.

Run one change at a time

Once you've identified the most likely bottleneck, choose an action that can test it.

For a capability gap, build and explain a relevant project. For a positioning gap, revise the resume around supported examples. For an employer constraint, explore suitable outside roles while keeping your current work professional.

Track what happens. Interviews, specific feedback, and clearer requirements are more useful than repeatedly changing your plan based on frustration.

Don't treat a small number of outcomes as a perfect diagnosis. Use them to decide the next investigation.

Build a plan that addresses the actual obstacle

A higher salary is a goal, not a syllabus. The work between your current situation and a better opportunity needs to be specific.
