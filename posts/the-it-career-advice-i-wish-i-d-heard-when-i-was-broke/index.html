The IT Career Advice I Wish I'd Heard When I Was Broke

When money is tight, start career planning with the smallest useful action—not a training purchase.

Identify the job and the actual gap before deciding what you need to buy. Better positioning, practical evidence, and a required credential are different problems with different costs.

I started from pizza delivery before building an IT career, so “invest in yourself” feels incomplete without that distinction. A useful plan should improve your options without assuming you can comfortably pay for every recommended course, exam, computer, or platform.

Keep immediate income needs separate from career development

You may need the job or side work that pays the bills now. That does not mean you lack ambition.

A career plan should fit around the reality of keeping yourself stable. Do not treat every hour spent earning necessary income as a failure to study.

At the same time, decide whether a small, repeatable block can be protected for a longer-term move. Even a modest block is more useful when it has a specific purpose than when it becomes another open-ended research session.

The plan should acknowledge constraints instead of pretending discipline removes them.

Match the expense to a specific career obstacle

Find a realistic role from your current starting point, then identify what is missing:

Obstacle A possible first action
Required credential Verify the requirement and the provider's actual process
Practical evidence Scope a small project using available equipment where possible
Unclear experience Rewrite truthful examples around the target responsibilities
Missing understanding Use relevant public documentation to investigate a defined question

If you're already in support, account management or access troubleshooting may connect to IAM. If you're new to IT, a broader first role may be more realistic.

The point is not that every useful step is free. It's that the purchase should follow the diagnosis.

Some gaps can be addressed initially with resources and experience you already have, rather than a larger program whose relevance you haven't established.

Build a small result before buying a larger promise

A small project can test whether the direction fits you before you commit to a long program.

For IAM, that might mean demonstrating a simple permission boundary, explaining a sign-in flow, or documenting a fictional employee's access changes.

Check licensing and hardware requirements first. A supposedly cheap lab can become expensive when it depends on a feature or machine you do not have.

The useful result is something you can explain and evaluate. It is not the feeling of progress that comes from gaining access to another course library.

Avoid turning someone else's success into your shopping list

A person who earns more may have taken certain exams, owned particular equipment, or followed a specific sequence. That does not establish that those purchases caused the result.

Their background, employer, timing, and responsibilities may differ from yours.

Borrow the questions they asked and the way they built evidence. Do not assume you need to recreate every item they bought.

The problem with following another person's certification roadmap is especially relevant when your budget leaves little room for experiments that do not serve a target.

Make the next step measurable without spending more

At the end of the week, you should be able to point to a clearer role target, a supported resume example, a finished test, or a relevant application.

Those are modest outcomes, but they change your position. “I watched more videos” may or may not.

If a paid resource genuinely solves the next problem, evaluate it against the alternatives and your budget. A low price alone does not make an unnecessary purchase useful.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook is a $9 guide to role selection, practical labs, and a focused plan. Its purpose is to reduce confusion for people considering IAM, not imply that buying a book creates a salary result.

The advice I wish more people gave is simple: protect your stability, choose a credible direction, and make the smallest useful move before committing to a larger expense.
