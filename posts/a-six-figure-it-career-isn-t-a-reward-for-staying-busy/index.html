A Six-Figure IT Career Isn't a Reward for Staying Busy

A full ticket queue doesn't tell you whether your work is preparing you for the responsibility and compensation you want next.

Look for evidence of growing capability, not just activity. Your effort matters, but more volume can consume time without changing the role you're qualified to pursue.

Choose one meaningful example and explain the problem, your contribution, and the verified result. Then compare that evidence with a suitable next job. That gives you a more useful development decision than assuming staying busy will eventually produce the career move.

Separate activity from the responsibility behind it

“Closed forty tickets” describes volume. It doesn't tell a reader what the tickets involved or what decisions you made.

A smaller number of cases might require deeper investigation, coordination, or ownership. A large queue might consist of tightly defined tasks with little room to change the process.

Neither category is worthless. They simply support different claims about your capability.

When you review your work, record the problem, your responsibility, and the result—not just the count.

Look for work that expands what you can explain

A useful development opportunity lets you understand more of the system or own a more complete outcome.

For example, you might move from collecting login symptoms to identifying which stage of the sign-in flow failed. Or from implementing approved access changes to documenting why the existing workflow produces repeated errors.

Those changes create stronger examples for future roles.

They don't automatically produce a raise. Compensation also depends on the employer, role, location, budget, and hiring conditions. The point is to improve the evidence you can take into those decisions.

Stop treating every urgent task as a career priority

Urgent work may be necessary without being developmental.

You still need to meet your job responsibilities. But outside those requirements, protect some time for a targeted skill or project rather than spending every available hour on whatever appears next.

Discuss development priorities with your manager where appropriate. An approved project with a clear outcome can be more useful than volunteering indefinitely for unrelated extra work.

If no relevant opportunity exists internally, build a bounded lab or investigate external roles while maintaining your current responsibilities.

Turn one piece of work into evidence of responsibility

Write a short account that answers:

  1. Problem: What did the user or business need?
  2. Contribution: What did you personally investigate, change, or coordinate?
  3. Constraints: Which limits or requirements shaped the work?
  4. Verification: How was the result checked?
  5. Boundary: Which parts remained outside your responsibility?

You don't need an invented financial impact or percentage improvement. Accurate operational evidence is more credible than an impressive number you can't support.

Keep the description specific enough to discuss in an interview, while excluding confidential details.

The exercise can also reveal a problem with the current role: increasing demands may be producing little new material you can demonstrate.

That doesn't automatically mean you should leave. It gives you a concrete question to discuss about assignments, development, or the next responsibility you need.

Compare your evidence with the next role

Look at actual responsibilities in suitable openings. If the role requires application integration and your examples only show routine account changes, you have identified a useful gap. If you already perform much of the work, your next step may be better positioning or applications rather than another course.

Use a check for learning that doesn't match your target before adding more study hours.

The comparison should lead to a choice, not another vague instruction to work harder.

Build a career plan that includes using the evidence

Learning and good performance need somewhere to go: a development conversation, a stronger application, an interview, or a decision to stay because the current tradeoff works for you.

Without that step, you can keep becoming more useful in one role while never testing your options elsewhere.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook connects an IT background to an IAM target, practical labs, and a 90-day action plan. The $9 guide is meant to organize effort around a move you can evaluate.

A six-figure goal doesn't require dismissing your current work. It requires being clear about which responsibilities and evidence could support the next opportunity.
