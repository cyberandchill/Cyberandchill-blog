Stop Buying IT Exams Before You've Picked a Job to Target

Before buying another exam voucher, write down the job it supports, the gap it addresses, and the result you expect from the study.

A deadline can help you follow a plan, but paying for an exam doesn't make the plan relevant. You can become committed to the wrong next step simply because you've already bought it.

Use the five checks below to justify the purchase before spending money. You don't need a salary prediction; you need a concrete reason this credential deserves priority now.

Name the role and the reason for the credential

Choose a target specific enough to evaluate, such as identity administration in an environment connected to your existing experience.

Then answer three questions:

  • Employer demand: Do suitable postings actually require or prefer the credential?
  • Learning value: Does its material cover responsibilities you need to understand?
  • Career fit: Is it relevant to the direction you've deliberately chosen?

Keep examples from the postings or official objectives beside your answers. That gives you something to reconsider when the next recommendation appears online.

“I should probably have it” isn't a strong reason. Neither is a high salary associated with people who hold it.

You can't know whether an exam will produce a job. You can decide whether it addresses a real requirement or skill gap, rather than merely making your professional profile feel more complete.

Identify the problem you expect it to solve

A credential can organize learning, support screening, or provide an external assessment of knowledge. It cannot automatically create experience with responsibilities you haven't performed.

Decide which problem matters most in your case.

If interviews repeatedly reveal that you cannot explain a sign-in flow, the response should include practical study and demonstration. If relevant postings consistently require a credential you lack, the exam may address a more direct obstacle.

Those situations might lead to the same purchase, but the preparation should look different. Knowing the purpose helps you avoid studying only to pass while leaving the original gap untouched.

Account for the time as well as the price

The voucher is only part of the commitment.

Consider the study time, practice environment, materials, and maintenance obligations relevant to the credential. Check current provider information rather than relying on an old cost estimate.

Then ask what that time would otherwise support: a relevant project, applications, interview practice, or a work responsibility you could develop with supervision.

This isn't a rule that projects always beat exams. It is a reminder that your time has competing uses. A sensible purchase fits the rest of the plan instead of replacing every other form of progress.

Define the evidence you will create alongside it

Choose a practical result connected to the material.

For an identity-focused credential, that could be a documented access scenario, a controlled troubleshooting exercise, or a lifecycle model with verified changes.

Make the result specific enough that you can tell whether it exists. “Learn the platform” is not a deliverable. “Explain and test this access rule using fictional identities” is.

The project gives you a way to assess understanding beyond recognition of exam answers. It also supplies something useful to discuss when an employer asks how you have applied the material.

Write the condition that would change your decision

Perhaps the target role changes, the credential's requirements don't fit your experience, or your research reveals a more important practical gap.

Name those possibilities before paying. This makes it easier to change course for a reason rather than continuing solely because the plan already feels familiar.

You can study relevant content without immediately buying the exam. You can also choose the exam confidently once the case is clear.

The point is to make the purchase serve a career decision you have already begun to understand.
