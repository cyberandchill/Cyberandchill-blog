What Your Last 100 Access Tickets Say About Your Next Career Move

Your recent access tickets can show which identity problems you already handle and where your responsibility stops.

You don't need to export company records or hit an exact sample of 100. Use only information you're authorized to review, follow workplace policies, and keep personal career notes generalized.

The goal is a pattern in your work—not a private collection of employer data. That pattern can help you choose an IAM specialty and a useful next project.

Classify the problem and your contribution separately

Use broad categories that don't require sensitive details:

Type of problem Example contribution to identify
Account lifecycle Gathered information or completed an approved change
Group or role assignment Checked approval, implemented, or verified access
Authentication recovery Followed verification and recovery procedures
Application access Investigated symptoms or coordinated escalation
Permissions troubleshooting Compared expected and observed access

One category may contain several levels of responsibility. Note whether you collected information, implemented a decision, investigated a failure, or owned the resolution.

Use an authorized review or your own non-sensitive recollection. The exact count matters less than being systematic enough to notice patterns.

This gives you a better starting point than saying you “work with users” and assuming the relevant identity experience will be obvious to an employer.

Separate frequent work from deep work

A task can occur often without requiring much independent judgment. A rare case may reveal a capability you should describe more clearly.

Mark the examples where you had to ask a useful question, compare evidence, identify an exception, or explain a result.

Don't assume the most common ticket category is automatically your best specialty. Consider both the volume and the type of reasoning involved.

The purpose is to understand your evidence, not to turn every routine action into an inflated achievement.

Identify where you consistently hand the work off

Your escalation boundary can reveal a useful development target.

Perhaps you can identify a sign-in failure but haven't learned how the identity provider and application interact. Perhaps you implement access changes but don't understand the approval or role-design process behind them.

Write the gap as a question you could investigate in a lab.

That is more actionable than deciding you need “more IAM experience” without knowing which part of the work is missing.

Look for problems that repeat because the process is unclear

Repeated requests may point to ambiguous roles, incomplete onboarding information, or a documentation gap.

In your current job, raise those observations through the appropriate owner rather than changing processes without authorization.

For personal learning, recreate the general problem with fictional data. A small scenario can demonstrate how better requirements or verification would improve the workflow.

Do not copy real tickets, names, internal systems, or screenshots into public materials, even when you intend to redact them later.

Connect the pattern to an IAM lane

A strong interest in investigating authentication failures may point toward identity operations or integration work. Interest in approvals and lifecycle decisions may point toward governance.

These are directions to investigate, not automatic conclusions about your personality or qualifications.

Compare current suitable postings and try a representative project. The work should appeal to you beyond the tasks you already know.

Use a comparison of IAM specializations to turn the pattern into a practical research choice.

Create one evidence statement and one next project

Summarize your current contribution in a truthful sentence, such as implementing approved access changes or preparing useful authentication escalations.

Then choose one gap to demonstrate in a fictional lab. Keep the scope small enough to finish and explain.

This gives the review an outcome. Without that step, you may produce a detailed inventory that doesn't change what you study or where you apply.

The next career clue may already be in the problems you handle. Look for the responsibility and the unanswered question—not just the ticket count.
