I Fell Into Identity and Access Management. Here's Why I Stayed.

I came to IAM through network support and security software, rather than choosing it at the start of my career.

I stayed because identity connected technical problems I already understood with work I wanted to learn more deeply. Accounts, devices, applications, and security controls began to fit into a more focused direction.

Finding a field by accident isn't a plan I'd recommend copying. Evaluating whether to stay is more useful: look at the problems, the workday, and whether your existing experience gives you a sensible starting point.

Identity connected systems I had already encountered

Accounts, devices, applications, and security controls don't live in separate boxes when someone needs to work.

A login problem can cross several of them.

That appealed to the part of me that wants to understand how a system fits together. The answer isn't always in the component that first appears broken.

Earlier troubleshooting experience had somewhere to go. I could build on it rather than treating specialization as a command to forget everything outside one product.

That doesn't mean broad experience automatically becomes deep identity expertise. There was more to learn.

It meant the next layer of learning had context.

Identity problems have a clear business purpose

One reason identity work appeals to me is that you can explain the need before naming the technology.

For example:

Business question Technical responsibility to investigate
Can this employee do their job? Appropriate access to the required application
Does someone retain unnecessary access? Accounts, assignments, permissions, and their justification
Can an administrator complete an approved task? Suitable privileges without granting everyone broad access

These questions give a configuration a purpose. They also help a customer-facing conversation stay focused on the person trying to accomplish something.

I value that connection between the technical setup and the outcome. It doesn't make every identity task exciting, but it provides a useful way to judge whether a change solves the right problem.

You can test whether you enjoy that reasoning before committing to the specialty.

Going deeper gave the learning more structure

General IT offers an endless supply of things to study.

A specialty helps you decide which unfamiliar topic is worth investigating next.

When the work concerns authentication and access, related concepts have a place: directories, trust, permissions, device context, application integration, and operational troubleshooting.

You still encounter adjacent systems. You just have a clearer reason for learning them.

For someone who likes understanding how things work, that can be more sustainable than chasing whichever technology appears most often in a feed.

The point is useful depth, not knowing the largest number of product names.

The career outcome mattered, but it wasn't the whole test

Specializing in identity became part of building my six-figure remote career.

That doesn't establish that every identity role pays the same, works remotely, or fits the same person.

I care about the responsibilities and the conditions around them. Schedule control, location flexibility, and a healthy working environment matter alongside income.

A specialty can give you a direction. You still need to evaluate the employer and the specific job.

I would rather investigate those details than sell myself on a field using a salary headline alone.

That applies even when the field is one I enjoy.

Make your own decision from a sample of the work

You don't need to repeat my career history to evaluate IAM.

Take a responsibility you've encountered—account management, application access, a login failure—and investigate the system behind it.

Build a small lab. Read a few roles that fit your experience. Pay attention to whether the actual questions interest you.

Also notice the less exciting parts: documentation, maintaining a configuration, handling exceptions, and coordinating a decision with someone else.

A useful career direction should survive contact with the ordinary work, not just the first interesting demonstration.

Use the next move to learn more

You can choose an initial direction without deciding what you'll do forever.

Start with one role category and one project. Let the result inform the next decision.
