You Can Be Great at IT and Still Be Badly Positioned for Better Pay

You can have relevant IT experience and still make it difficult for an employer to see why you fit an identity role.

Your resume needs to connect your experience to the work you're applying to do. A list of Windows, networking, cloud, and security products doesn't make that connection by itself.

Compare “I have broad IT experience” with “I troubleshoot employee access issues and have built a lab showing group-based permissions.” The second introduction gives a hiring team something specific to evaluate without claiming more experience.

Lead with the identity work relevant to the job

You don't have to erase the breadth of your background.

You do need to decide what a particular application is supposed to communicate.

For an identity administration role, lead with relevant account, access, troubleshooting, or directory work you have actually done. Keep unrelated experience available, but don't make it compete for the first sentence.

Think of this as choosing the order of evidence.

A hiring team shouldn't have to infer your interest in identity from a long inventory of technologies. State the direction, then support it.

Changing the order won't create missing capability. It will make existing capability easier to evaluate.

Back up each skill claim with a concrete example

“I'm strong at troubleshooting” gives an employer a claim, but no way to assess it. Describe an access problem, what you investigated, and how you checked the result.

Use the same test for your other skills:

Skill you mention Evidence that helps explain it
Troubleshooting A real investigation or a clearly labeled lab failure and correction
Documentation A readable explanation someone else could follow
Automation What the process changed and how you tested the output
Customer communication Your actual responsibility and how you explained the issue

A small example is enough when it supports a narrow claim. You don't need to invent an enterprise project to sound credible.

If you don't have an example, narrow the claim or build the missing experience. Adding another adjective won't solve that problem.

Keep your resume, profile, and project page consistent

These surfaces don't need identical wording. They should describe the same person.

Suppose your resume emphasizes identity administration, your profile opens with offensive security, and your portfolio contains only unrelated networking diagrams.

That might reflect genuine interests. It also leaves the reader unsure which work you're pursuing.

For a particular search, choose a primary direction. Reorder the relevant material so it is easy to find.

You can remain interested in other fields without presenting them all as your immediate career objective.

A specific search is not a lifelong promise.

Write a two-sentence introduction

Try this structure in your own words. The first sentence identifies your relevant background. The second describes the work you're moving toward and the evidence you've built.

For example: “My background is in enterprise IT support, including account and application access troubleshooting. I'm pursuing identity administration roles and have built a lab covering permissions, sign-in troubleshooting, and employee access changes.”

Only use details that are true for you.

Read it aloud. Remove words you wouldn't say in a normal conversation. Then ask whether someone unfamiliar with your history could repeat what you do and what you want.

That is a better test than whether the wording sounds impressive.

Don't confuse clearer presentation with higher market value

A well-written profile can help communicate fit. It cannot guarantee better compensation or replace the experience an employer genuinely needs.

Sometimes the missing piece is technical depth. Sometimes the opportunity doesn't match your location or constraints. Sometimes there simply isn't a fit.

Clear positioning still helps because it makes the feedback easier to interpret.

When you're explicit about the role and your evidence, you have a better chance of learning whether the gap is in the work, the presentation, or the opportunity you're pursuing.

That is much more actionable than vaguely deciding you need to “improve your brand.”
