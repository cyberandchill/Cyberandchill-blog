Where to Put Home Lab Projects on an IT Resume

Put a home lab where it adds relevant evidence to your resume, and label it clearly as a personal or training project.

Don't present it as employment or let it bury stronger professional experience. Its placement depends on the gap it fills for the role you're applying to do.

If the lab supplies your main identity example, make it easy to find. If your workplace responsibilities already establish that capability, lead with them. In either case, describe the project through a tested result rather than an installation inventory.

Use a clearly labeled projects section

A heading such as “Selected IAM Projects” or “Independent Technical Projects” makes the context understandable.

Include the project name, a concise description, and a link to the evidence when the link is useful. Keep the name focused on the problem, not only the product installed.

For example: “Group-Based Access Validation Lab” is more informative than “My Windows Server.”

Do not list a fictional company as an employer. If the scenario uses a fictional organization, say so in the project description or linked writeup.

Put projects higher when they supply your main identity evidence

Someone with support experience but little professional identity ownership may benefit from a visible project section near the top half of the resume.

The projects can show relevant learning that the employment history does not yet establish.

That does not mean the lab becomes equivalent to production experience. It means it answers a specific question about your developing capability.

Choose one or two relevant projects rather than listing every installation. The section should make the transition easier to understand, not create another wall of unrelated tools.

Keep professional experience first when it is stronger

If you already manage relevant identity responsibilities at work, those examples may deserve more space than a basic home lab.

Use the project to demonstrate a new area, a different tool, or a skill you cannot discuss publicly from your employment.

A lab should complement the career story. It should not make an experienced candidate appear to have only classroom-style evidence.

The right order depends on the target role and the relative strength of the examples, not a universal resume template.

Write a project bullet around the environment and tests

A useful project bullet identifies three things:

Element What to include
Environment A clearly labeled isolated or personal lab
Work performed The scenario you configured and your contribution
Evidence The allowed, denied, or changed-state results you actually verified

For example: “Built an isolated directory-access scenario using fictional users; verified permitted read access, denial for a comparison account, and the result after membership removal.”

Use that wording only when those tests happened.

Using lab evidence without pretending it was production experience means avoiding unsupported enterprise scale, customer impact, or operational ownership.

The example can still be valuable. It gives an interviewer a concrete way to assess what you understand while keeping the conditions under which you learned it clear.

Make the linked page worth opening

The project page should explain the business scenario, your contribution, the key configuration, the tests, and the limitations.

Keep sensitive information out of screenshots and repositories. Use fictional data rather than trying to sanitize an employer environment after the fact.

A resume link should lead directly to the relevant project or a clear portfolio index. Do not make the reader search through an unrelated profile to find the evidence mentioned in the bullet.

Remove the project when it no longer adds useful evidence

As your professional experience grows, an introductory lab may become less important. Keep a fuller archive, but let the application version of the resume reflect the strongest current case.
