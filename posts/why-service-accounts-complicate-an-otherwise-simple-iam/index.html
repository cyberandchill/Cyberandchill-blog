Why Service Accounts Complicate an Otherwise Simple IAM Environment

A service account may keep a task running long after its original creator changes jobs or leaves.

Identify the current owner, purpose, dependencies, permissions, and recovery needs—not just the account name.

Workload identities raise different operational questions from employee accounts. A credential change can interrupt a service, and stopping a process doesn't necessarily remove its entitlement. The account may be non-human, but responsibility for it still needs to be assigned and maintained.

Start with a reporting task, not an account name

Imagine a fictional reporting process that reads a test data source and writes a daily summary to a separate location.

The account's purpose should be that specific. “Service account for IT” tells you very little about what it should be allowed to do.

Record the workload, the systems it uses, the required actions, and the team responsible for it. If the process only needs to read one source and write one output, broad administrative access needs a much better justification than convenience.

The scenario gives you a way to discuss permissions without treating the account as a generic exception.

Map dependencies before changing credentials

A password change that is routine for a person can interrupt a process when the same credential is stored in a task, application configuration, or another dependency.

Before making an authorized change, identify where the account is used and who can validate the service afterward. In a lab, keep the dependencies deliberately small so you can observe the result safely.

The important skill is not remembering where to click “reset password.” It's understanding the effect of the change and planning how to verify the workload still performs its approved function.

Don't test this by modifying an unknown account in a real environment.

Use an identity type that fits the platform

Not every workload should use a traditional user account with a manually managed password.

Microsoft documents managed service accounts for Windows, including automatic password management for supported scenarios. In Azure, managed identities can remove the need for application code to manage credentials when accessing supported resources.

Those are different mechanisms for different environments. Neither removes the need to assign appropriate permissions, understand dependencies, and maintain ownership.

For a first project, explain the choice and its limits. You don't need to deploy every identity type to show that you understand why the choice matters.

Give the workload an approval, review, and retirement process

A process shouldn't retain access indefinitely because nobody remembers why it exists.

Define:

  • Who approves its purpose and permissions.
  • Who reviews whether those permissions remain appropriate.
  • How ownership transfers when a person or team changes.
  • What happens when the process is retired.

Use a fictional reporting task to test retirement. Stop the expected execution, remove the relevant access through the approved lab process, and verify that the identity can no longer reach the sample resource.

Keep two results separate: the task stopped running and the identity lost the underlying entitlement. One doesn't automatically prove the other.

Also document how you'd identify an ownerless or obsolete workload. The lifecycle should make responsibility visible before a forgotten dependency turns an apparently simple account change into a service problem.

Keep human and workload controls distinct

A background process cannot always respond to the same interactive checks as a person. Don't assume the controls you use for employee sign-in transfer unchanged to every service identity.

Microsoft's workload identity overview distinguishes applications, service principals, and managed identities from human identities.

For your project, explain the identity, authentication method, permission scope, owner, and retirement condition. That is more useful than attaching a vague “non-human identity” label to an account.
