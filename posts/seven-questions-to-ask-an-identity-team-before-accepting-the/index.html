Seven Questions to Ask an Identity Team Before Accepting the Job

Before accepting an identity job, ask what you'll own, how the team operates, and what support exists when something fails.

The title, salary, and product list don't establish whether the opportunity fits you. Concrete examples of work and expectations do.

Use these seven questions to open a discussion, not to rush through an interview checklist. When an answer is vague, ask what it looks like in an ordinary week or a recent project.

1. What should this person accomplish in the first three months?

This reveals whether the team has a concrete reason for the opening.

Listen for achievable outcomes, onboarding expectations, and the distinction between learning the environment and independently owning high-impact work. Ask which result matters most if the list is long.

A role can involve uncertainty without being poorly planned. What you need to understand is how priorities will be chosen and who helps resolve them.

The answer also gives you a useful way to compare your current evidence with the responsibility ahead.

2. Which identity processes would I own, and which would I support?

Ask about application integrations, access requests, lifecycle changes, governance, and authentication investigations where relevant.

Ownership means different things in different teams. Clarify whether you would make design decisions, implement approved changes, or coordinate work controlled elsewhere.

This protects you from assuming that a broad title includes authority the role does not actually have. It also helps you identify where you will need to collaborate rather than work independently.

3. What does a typical week contain?

Ask for a recent example of the balance between planned projects, operational requests, incidents, documentation, and meetings.

You are trying to picture the ordinary workday, not just the most interesting initiative on the roadmap.

Follow up on interruptions and shifting priorities. A role advertised around engineering can still carry substantial operational work. That isn't automatically bad, but you should understand it before deciding whether it supports your goals.

4. How are changes reviewed, tested, and recovered?

Identity changes can affect users' access. Ask how the team makes those changes responsibly.

Listen for:

  • Review: Who checks the proposed change and its intended effect?
  • Testing: What validation is available before and after implementation?
  • Recovery: How is a failed change handled?
  • Support: What help does a new employee have when the situation is unfamiliar?

You aren't asking for confidential configurations. You are trying to understand the operating process and how responsibility is shared.

“We trust people to know what they're doing” may sound empowering, but it doesn't explain review or escalation. Ask for a non-sensitive example.

Independence is useful when it comes with clear authority and expectations. It shouldn't require guessing about the organization's acceptable risk or discovering the recovery plan only after a change fails.

5. What are the on-call and response expectations?

Clarify the rotation, coverage, escalation, and expectations outside normal hours. Ask how often the team actually encounters interruptions, while recognizing that a past period does not guarantee the future.

Don't assume a remote role has flexible hours or that identity work is automatically quiet.

For a deeper discussion, use specific questions about on-call work. This is a working-condition issue worth understanding before the offer becomes emotionally difficult to decline.

6. Where does the team most need improvement?

This question can reveal documentation gaps, unclear ownership, repetitive work, or integration challenges.

Ask how the team plans to address the issue and what support the new hire will have. A candid answer is not necessarily a warning sign; every environment has work to improve.

The important distinction is between a manageable problem with ownership and an expectation that one person will absorb unlimited unresolved responsibility.

7. How will success be evaluated?

Ask how the manager distinguishes good performance from simply staying busy.

Look for expectations that connect to the role's actual responsibilities. Clarify how feedback is given and how priorities can change during the year.

Then compare the answers with your goals for pay, work content, schedule, and development. Mark unresolved questions instead of filling them with optimistic assumptions.
