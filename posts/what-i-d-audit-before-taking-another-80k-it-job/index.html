What I'd Audit Before Taking Another $80K IT Job

Another $80K IT job could be a good move if it improves your responsibilities, stability, schedule, or future options. The salary staying the same doesn't tell you whether the opportunity is better.

It could also leave you doing nearly identical work with the same career frustrations at a different company.

Before accepting, compare the actual role with the reason you're leaving. Audit the work, support, conditions, and experience you could reasonably gain—not just the title and interview promises.

Compare the actual work with your current work

Ask what you would do in a normal week and what you would be expected to own after several months.

Would you gain responsibility for a system, a project, or a more complex class of problems? Or would you process a larger version of the same queue?

Neither answer is automatically bad. The important point is whether it fits your reason for moving.

If your goal is an identity specialization, identify the specific IAM responsibilities available in the role. “Exposure to security” is too vague to evaluate.

Ask for evidence of the learning opportunity

A promise that you will “learn a lot” can mean structured mentorship. It can also mean being left alone with unfamiliar responsibilities.

Ask who reviews technical work, how changes are approved, and what support exists when a problem exceeds your experience.

Request examples of projects people in the role have actually completed. Find out whether you would participate in design, implementation, testing, or only the administrative cleanup afterward.

A learning opportunity should describe the work and support, not just the number of products in the environment.

Compare the full employment arrangement

Review guaranteed compensation, variable compensation, benefits, travel, on-call expectations, and location requirements.

Treat uncertain pay as uncertain. Clarify whether remote work is an established arrangement or an informal exception that could change.

A role with a familiar salary can still be materially better or worse once those conditions are included. Keep your own non-negotiables visible so you do not rationalize them away because you are eager to leave.

For contractual or jurisdiction-specific questions, obtain appropriate professional advice rather than relying on a verbal interpretation during recruitment.

Look for a credible path to broader responsibility

Ask what successful performance looks like and how the role could develop.

You are looking for concrete possibilities, not a guaranteed promotion date. A defined integration project, access-review initiative, or identity troubleshooting responsibility is more informative than “there is plenty of room to grow.”

Also ask what might prevent that growth. A small team may genuinely have limited room for a specialist role. A larger team may have opportunities but rigid boundaries around who can take them on.

Understanding the constraint lets you make an informed trade.

Ask what you could truthfully describe after a year

Imagine writing your next application after spending a year in the proposed role.

Would you be able to describe:

  • A new area of responsibility?
  • Relevant identity or security problems you helped resolve?
  • Decisions or investigations you can explain in depth?
  • Experience that supports the specialty you want next?
  • Better working conditions that matter enough to justify the move?

You cannot predict the year precisely. Use the questions to test whether the opportunity plausibly serves your goals.

A role may be worthwhile for stability or flexibility even without rapid technical growth. Make that trade deliberately rather than expecting every change of employer to produce career progression.

A promotion can also move you away from your intended specialty. Compare the work you would gain with the work you want to become better at.

Make the reason for accepting explicit

Write a short decision note: “I would accept because this role improves ___, gives me responsibility for ___, and fits my constraints around ___.”

Then list the unresolved questions. Do not let a general feeling of momentum stand in for answers to material concerns.
