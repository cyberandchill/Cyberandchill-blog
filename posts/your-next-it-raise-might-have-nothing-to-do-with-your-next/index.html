Your Next IT Raise Might Have Nothing to Do With Your Next Certification

Another certification may improve your knowledge without changing your salary. Find out what would trigger different compensation before treating an exam as your raise strategy.

The answer might involve promotion criteria, a different responsibility, an available position, or an external move. Those are separate from whether the study itself is useful.

You can pursue both learning and better pay, but give each a clear purpose. Otherwise, you may keep meeting study goals while waiting for a compensation change nobody has actually committed to.

Identify whether you're asking for performance pay or different work

A performance increase rewards how you're doing within an existing role. A promotion or role change may involve a different scope of responsibility. An external move introduces another employer's needs and compensation structure.

Those are different conversations. If you want recognition for strong current performance, prepare evidence of that performance. If you want a role with broader responsibilities, show why you're ready to own them. If your employer has no suitable path, research what other roles value your experience.

A new credential may support one of these cases. It doesn't replace the need to identify which case you're making.

Ask your manager for concrete criteria

A vague promise to “keep developing” is difficult to use as a plan.

Ask what responsibilities distinguish the next level, what evidence would demonstrate readiness, and how the decision process works. Find out whether there is an actual role or opportunity, not merely a general preference for ambitious employees.

Keep the conversation professional and specific. You are trying to understand the path, not demand a guarantee about a future outcome.

Write down what you learn. If the criteria remain unclear, treat that uncertainty as information rather than filling the gap with your own assumption that one more exam will solve it.

Build evidence tied to the proposed responsibility

Suppose the next role involves owning a defined identity service rather than processing routine account requests.

Relevant evidence could include deeper troubleshooting, careful change execution, useful documentation, and the ability to recognize when another owner must make a decision. The exact evidence depends on the role.

Seek authorized opportunities to develop those responsibilities. Don't expand privileges or make changes without approval to create a stronger promotion story.

A personal lab can support your learning, but professional ownership and lab practice should remain clearly distinguished in the conversation.

Compare outside roles without turning the search into a threat

External research can help you judge whether your current path fits. Compare the whole opportunity:

  • Responsibilities: What would you own, and which gaps would you need to close?
  • Compensation: What is guaranteed, variable, or dependent on conditions?
  • Schedule and location: What do remote work, travel, and availability mean in practice?
  • Development: What support or experience would help you progress?

You don't need to announce every application or use another employer's salary as leverage in a confrontation. The immediate goal is information.

If the internal path is limited, you will have a clearer basis for deciding whether to leave. If an outside offer has worse conditions, you can recognize that too.

A higher headline number doesn't automatically make a move better. Evaluate the actual work and its uncertainties alongside the pay.

Give learning and compensation separate checkpoints

Track what your study produces: better understanding, a completed project, or a credential relevant to a target. Then separately track what changes in your career options or responsibilities.

This prevents you from declaring the learning worthless because a raise didn't immediately follow. It also prevents endless learning from hiding a compensation path that never becomes clearer.

You can value education and still ask whether your current career strategy is working.

A useful plan names the next responsibility and the evidence required for it. It also names who can make the compensation decision, so progress isn't measured against a promise nobody actually made.
