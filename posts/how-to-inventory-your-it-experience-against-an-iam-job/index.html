How to Inventory Your IT Experience Against an IAM Job Posting

Before tailoring an IAM resume, match one piece of evidence to each important responsibility in the actual posting.

Use responsibilities as the rows, not every acronym you've encountered. This reveals relevant strengths, honest gaps, and the practical work that could improve the application.

Keep the inventory safe and specific. It should help you select and explain experience, not become a personal copy of employer tickets or sensitive configuration records.

Copy the responsibility in plain language

Start with a job you would genuinely consider, including its location, working conditions, and required level of experience.

Turn broad requirements into understandable tasks. “Identity lifecycle” might involve creating, changing, and removing access as someone's relationship with the organization changes. “SSO support” might involve investigating application sign-in failures.

Keep the original wording nearby so you don't accidentally simplify away an important requirement.

When the description is vague, mark it as a question rather than inventing the employer's expectations.

Record what you did and what another person owned

For each requirement, write the closest genuine example and separate:

  • Situation: What needed to happen?
  • Contribution: What did you personally do?
  • Boundary: Which decision or action belonged to someone else?
  • Evidence: What supports the result you describe?

“The team deployed MFA” doesn't establish whether you configured policy, supported enrollment, investigated failures, or documented recovery. Those are different contributions, each potentially relevant to a different role.

Use general, non-sensitive descriptions rather than copying private tickets or customer information into the worksheet. Follow confidentiality requirements.

The inventory becomes useful when it helps you choose an accurate sentence and prepare for follow-up questions. It should not turn participation in a large project into ownership of every part of that project.

Label the depth of the evidence

Use a small set of categories: performed independently, performed with support, observed, practiced in a lab, studied, or not yet learned.

The categories are for clarity, not self-judgment. They prevent you from presenting a lab as production work or overlooking useful experience because you weren't the senior person involved.

Add a sentence about what you can explain now. If you performed a task long ago but no longer understand the mechanism, that is a different preparation need from having never encountered it.

Identify the proof you can share safely

Professional evidence may be a clear interview example rather than a public artifact. Lab evidence may include a diagram, test table, and sanitized documentation.

Write what supports the claim and where it belongs: resume bullet, interview story, projects section, or a future learning task.

The rules for using lab evidence without implying production experience are important when one responsibility is supported by different kinds of experience.

You don't need to publish everything to make it useful in an application.

Assign one next action to the important gaps

Avoid turning every blank row into a new course purchase.

Some gaps need a practical exercise. Others need refreshed understanding, a clearer example, or a question to ask the employer. A genuine mandatory requirement may mean the role isn't the right target yet.

For example, “never configured SSO” could lead to a small test integration. “Can't explain my MFA contribution clearly” may require reviewing your own role and writing an accurate summary, not rebuilding an entire lab.

Prioritize the gaps that affect the central responsibilities.

Use the inventory to edit the application

Choose the strongest relevant examples for the resume and prepare deeper explanations for interviews.

Do not paste the entire worksheet into your application. It is a decision tool, not the final document.

After applying, update it with useful feedback. If a conversation reveals a different responsibility than the posting suggested, adjust your preparation rather than defending an outdated interpretation.

Over time, the inventory becomes a clearer record of your readiness than a list of courses completed.

Start with what you can already support. Then make the missing evidence specific enough that you can do something about it.
