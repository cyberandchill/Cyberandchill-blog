The IT Skills You're Proud Of Might Not Be the Ones That Raise Your Pay

The skill you're proudest of may not be the strongest evidence for your next job.

Separate personal achievement from relevance to the responsibilities being hired for. That doesn't make the skill unimportant or the time spent learning it wasted.

It helps you decide where the example belongs and what to learn next. A future employer needs to understand how your capability supports this role, not rank every technical accomplishment you've ever accumulated.

Name the achievement without defending it

Write down the project, certification, or technical capability you keep leading with.

Then ask what it demonstrates: persistence, troubleshooting, design, automation, platform knowledge, or something else.

This helps you find the transferable value without insisting that the exact technology must be central to every future job.

You can remain proud of the work while changing how prominently it appears in an application. A resume is a communication tool, not a permanent memorial to the effort you spent learning.

Match the achievement to the responsibility it supports

Read the main duties in suitable postings, then evaluate your examples.

Ask:

  1. Which responsibility does this achievement demonstrate?
  2. Is that responsibility central to this role?
  3. Is another example a clearer, more direct match?
  4. Where could the less direct example still support the story?

For a lifecycle-focused IAM position, a small example of approved account changes and verification may be more relevant than a complex networking build.

The networking work can still demonstrate investigation or dependency reasoning. It may simply belong later in the application.

Don't make the employer infer a connection you could explain with a better example. Lead with the evidence that answers the role's questions, while keeping other achievements available for conversations where their value is clearer.

Watch for learning driven mainly by familiarity

It is easy to keep getting better at the subject you already enjoy while postponing the unfamiliar gap that limits the next move.

For an IAM pivot, that gap might be application integration, governance concepts, or explaining a sign-in flow. More work in the familiar area may not close it.

This is not a demand to abandon your strengths.

Use them as a foundation, then choose one adjacent capability that makes the career story more complete. The uncomfortable subject may be the useful next step precisely because it isn't already easy.

Distinguish a hobby from a career requirement

You are allowed to build things simply because they interest you.

The problem begins when every enjoyable project is presented to yourself as necessary career preparation. That can make it difficult to notice that the application, interview practice, or relevant lab is still unfinished.

Keep a separate curiosity list if that helps.

Use a deliberate limit on trying to learn every interesting technology to preserve enjoyment without letting it continually replace the work connected to your target role.

Reposition the strongest relevant examples first

Choose the evidence that most directly answers the employer's questions.

A smaller project with a clear requirement, tests, and explanation may deserve more space than a larger project whose relevance is indirect. Keep the larger achievement available where it adds useful context.

Do not invent outcomes to make the preferred project seem relevant.

If the connection is weak, acknowledge that and build a better example. Career positioning works best when it reveals real fit rather than decorating a mismatch with stronger language.

Decide what to learn next with a specific reason

Finish the sentence: “This activity should help me demonstrate this responsibility for this kind of role.”

If the connection is clear, make it part of the plan. If it is mainly personal interest, enjoy it without pretending it is the only thing standing between you and a better job.

Both kinds of learning can belong in your life, but they should not be confused.
