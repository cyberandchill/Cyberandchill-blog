Why a Better Commute Doesn't Fix a Stalled Career

A shorter commute can improve your life without resolving a stalled career. Treat the location benefit and the development opportunity as separate parts of the decision.

You don't need one job change to solve everything. You do need to know which problem it addresses.

If the move gives you time or energy for a deliberate next step, that can be useful. Don't assume the improvement in travel automatically means the responsibilities or future options improved too.

Identify which problem the new location actually solves

Before comparing jobs, name what isn't working now.

Possible problems include:

  • Too much travel time.
  • An unsuitable schedule.
  • Limited technical growth.
  • Responsibilities you no longer want.
  • Compensation that doesn't meet your goals.
  • No concrete development opportunity.

A nearby job may fix the commute and leave the rest unchanged. A remote role may remove travel while adding constant availability requirements.

Decide which tradeoff matters most right now. Relief in one area can be worthwhile without needing to call it progress in every area.

Then give the remaining career problem its own plan. If you still want a specialized role, identify what learning, evidence, or applications will move you toward it rather than expecting the better commute to create that direction on its own.

Compare the work after removing the location benefit

Imagine both jobs were equally convenient to reach. Which role would you prefer based on responsibilities, support, working conditions, and future options?

That thought experiment exposes the value of the work itself.

A role with similar duties may still be a good choice when the commute improvement is large enough for you. But make the tradeoff consciously rather than describing the move as a technical promotion when it isn't.

Ask what you would be able to demonstrate after a year in the new position that you cannot demonstrate now.

Decide what the recovered time is for

Time saved doesn't have to become study time. Rest, exercise, relationships, and a less exhausting day are legitimate reasons to prefer a job.

When career development is also a goal, assign a realistic portion of that time to it. Don't create a schedule that assumes every saved minute will become perfect concentration.

For example, you might protect two short study sessions and one weekend lab block. Give those sessions a specific IAM responsibility to explore.

The five-hours-a-week approach to upskilling can help you turn available time into a manageable plan rather than a new source of guilt.

Check whether the new environment supports the plan

A convenient location won't help your learning if the role regularly consumes your evenings or leaves you unable to recover.

Ask about workload, escalation patterns, change windows, and expectations outside scheduled hours. Look at the whole week rather than the distance from home.

Also ask about development within the job. An approved opportunity to document an access process or work alongside an identity team may be more useful than simply having a nearby office.

Get specific about what is available and who supports it. Vague promises are hard to build a plan around.

Use a review date instead of an open-ended hope

When you make a move primarily for lifestyle reasons, set a point to review whether it is also supporting your longer-term direction.

By that date, you might aim to have chosen an IAM lane, completed a focused project, or tested your application materials against relevant roles.

The review is not a demand to leave again. It's a way to notice whether the extra flexibility is creating the options you expected.

Without it, a comfortable arrangement can quietly become another year of intending to study later.

Let a good job improve your life without inventing a career story

Sometimes the right decision is to take the less exhausting job and stabilize your routine. You don't have to justify that choice with a promised salary jump.

Then build the career plan separately, based on the work you want and the skills you need to demonstrate.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook gives you a $9 structure for exploring IAM, building relevant labs, and working through a 90-day plan.

A better commute can give you room to use that plan. Just be clear about the difference between gaining time and deciding what to do with it.
