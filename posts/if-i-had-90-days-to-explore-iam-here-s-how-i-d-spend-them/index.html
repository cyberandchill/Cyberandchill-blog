If I Had 90 Days to Explore IAM, Here's How I'd Spend Them

Use ninety days to choose an IAM direction, build relevant examples, and test your fit with actual employers.

The goal is better evidence and better career decisions—not a guaranteed job within three months. You'll learn more from a few completed projects and applications than from an expanding collection of course bookmarks.

The plan below fits around a full-time job and gives each stage a practical output. Apply earlier when a suitable opening matches your current experience; the timeline is a planning aid, not a rule that delays your search.

Days 1–14: choose the role and the first gap

Save a small number of jobs you could realistically accept.

Read the responsibilities. Separate genuine requirements from preferences, and compare the work with your background.

Choose one main direction. You can explore another later.

Create an experience inventory using real examples. Mark which responsibilities you've performed, which you've practiced in a lab, and which you only understand in theory.

Then select one project that addresses a meaningful gap.

By the end of this phase, you should have a target, a manageable project brief, and scheduled work blocks. You do not need an elaborate career dashboard.

Days 15–30: finish one directory-access project

For a common starting point, build a small directory-access scenario with fictional users and a resource.

Write down who should have access before configuring it. Test both the permitted and unpermitted cases. Record what happens after an access change.

Keep the environment isolated and recoverable. Use ordinary test identities for the access checks.

Publish a short explanation with the requirement, configuration, test results, and limitations. Don't wait until you have an entire portfolio to make the first project readable.

When a different project better matches your target role, do that first instead. The schedule serves the role, not the other way around.

Days 31–45: practice one sign-in integration

Connect a test application to an identity provider using supported documentation.

Explain the successful path. Then introduce one controlled, reversible configuration problem in the lab and diagnose it.

Record the symptom, evidence, hypothesis, correction, and retest.

You are trying to build a troubleshooting story you understand. A working setup copied from a tutorial without an explanation isn't the finish line.

Keep real tokens and credentials out of your public material.

If the integration expands beyond your available time, reduce the scope before adding another platform.

Days 46–60: model an employee lifecycle

Create a fictional joiner, mover, and leaver scenario.

Show which access should exist at each stage, who approves the change, and how you verify it.

Pay special attention to removing obsolete access during the transfer. Include an exception or failed step you can explain.

You can model the process manually before automating it. Automation is useful when you understand the decisions and can test the resulting state.

By this point, the projects should demonstrate different capabilities rather than repeat the same setup with different names.

Days 61–75: make projects and applications easy to evaluate

Review each project as though you'd never seen it before. Then make these improvements:

  1. Clarify the opening: Put the problem, your contribution, and the result before the installation diary.
  2. Organize the evidence: Keep detailed notes available without making them the first thing a reader sees.
  3. Update career materials: Add truthful examples relevant to your target role on your resume and profile.
  4. Practice the explanation: Walk through one project without reading its instructions.

Ask a peer to challenge a design choice or ask what would change in a larger environment. Use the questions to improve your explanation, not invent production experience.

You should already be applying when appropriate roles appear and your experience is relevant. Day 61 is simply a checkpoint for improving the material; it isn't permission you must wait for.

Days 76–90: use feedback to choose the next move

Review what happened. No useful responses may justify another look at targeting and presentation. Repeated technical questions you struggle with point toward specific study. Discovering that the work doesn't appeal to you is also information worth using.

Don't add a new certification simply because the calendar ended.

Choose the next action from the evidence you've collected.

Some people will need more time for the projects. Others will already be qualified for parts of the role and can focus on applications sooner.
