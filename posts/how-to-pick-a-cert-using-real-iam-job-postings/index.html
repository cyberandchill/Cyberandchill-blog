How to Pick a Cert Using Real IAM Job Postings

Use real IAM job postings to decide which certification deserves your time. The best starting point is a job you'd actually accept, not the most impressive credential on someone else's roadmap.

Choose a small group of roles that fit your location, work preferences, and experience. Then separate what employers want you to know from the badges they name.

That comparison gives your study plan a purpose. You can assess whether an exam closes a relevant gap instead of buying it because you're unsure what else to do.

Remove the roles you wouldn't accept

Start with practical constraints. Location, work authorization, travel, schedule, and employment type can eliminate a role before the technology matters.

Also remove postings that describe a level of responsibility far beyond the move you're evaluating. Studying an architect's entire requirement list can make a realistic administration role look unnecessarily distant.

Keep a small, coherent sample. Five relevant postings are more useful for this exercise than fifty unrelated jobs containing the word “identity.” The number is just a manageable starting point, not a statistical claim about the market.

Record when you reviewed them because postings change.

Separate certification requirements from hands-on duties

Create two columns while reading each posting:

Credentials named Work the employer expects
Required or preferred certifications Application administration, access changes, authentication troubleshooting, or other duties
Exact wording and any active-status requirement The depth of responsibility and experience requested

Keep the employer's language available so you can check your interpretation later. “Preferred” is not the same as “required,” and neither column replaces the other.

Passing an exam doesn't demonstrate that you've performed every duty in the posting. Likewise, a credential that isn't named may still provide a useful structure for learning relevant concepts.

Compare the actual material with the work. Counting mentions can show a pattern in your small sample, but it isn't enough to decide which exam will address your particular gap.

Group repeated responsibilities

Look for recurring work across your sample. You might see directory administration, access reviews, application sign-in, or scripting. Group related requirements without pretending every product does the same thing.

Now mark what you can already demonstrate. A simple scale works: done in production, demonstrated in a lab, studied only, or not yet understood.

You are building a decision aid for yourself. Be stricter than you would be when trying to win an argument.

The exercise should reveal a few meaningful gaps rather than make your experience look perfect.

Compare the gap with current official objectives

Open the certification provider's current outline. For example, Microsoft's SC-300 study guide describes the identity and access administration topics assessed by that exam. Check the effective date when more than one version is listed. Source: Microsoft SC-300 study guide.

Map relevant objectives to the gaps you identified.

A useful match gives you a reason to study. A weak match gives you a reason to look elsewhere—or to do a project instead.

Don't count a certification as valuable just because it contains one familiar keyword. Consider how much of the study effort contributes to work you actually need.

Write a one-paragraph purchase decision

Before spending money, summarize the case. “I am targeting these responsibilities. These are the gaps I need to close. This certification covers a useful portion of them. I can practice the material in this environment. I will pair it with this project.”

That paragraph should also identify what the exam will not solve.

Maybe you still need relevant work experience, a better portfolio explanation, or applications to employers with suitable openings.

When the case is unclear, you haven't failed. You've saved yourself from turning uncertainty into a purchase.

Recheck after you get feedback

Your first sample will be imperfect. Applications, interviews, and conversations may reveal that you misunderstood a role or overlooked an important responsibility. Update your plan instead of defending the original choice forever.
