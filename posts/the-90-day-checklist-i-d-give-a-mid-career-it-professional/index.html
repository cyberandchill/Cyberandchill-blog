The 90-Day Checklist I'd Give a Mid-Career IT Professional

A useful 90-day career plan gives you four things to produce: a credible job target, a tested project, a clear application story, and feedback that guides the next step.

It should not promise that everyone starts with the same skills or receives an offer on the same timetable.

Use the checkpoints to make decisions. If you're already a reasonable match for a role, apply before the plan ends. If a gap becomes clear, adjust the work instead of protecting the original schedule.

Days 1–14: Establish a target you can explain

Save several roles that fit your experience and practical constraints.

Compare the responsibilities rather than collecting every product name. Identify the work you have done professionally, the work you have only studied, and one important gap you can explore.

Your checkpoint evidence is a short role map: target responsibility, relevant example, missing capability, and next action.

Done means you can explain why this direction makes sense for you. It does not mean you have selected a permanent specialty or finished every foundational lesson.

Days 15–30: Finish one project with testable evidence

Choose a bounded project that addresses a requirement in your target role: directory access, a test SSO integration, or a fictional employee lifecycle change.

Before calling it finished, check:

  1. Requirement: Have you stated what should be allowed or prevented?
  2. Baseline: Can you show the starting configuration and expected behavior?
  3. Test: Have you recorded a meaningful allowed, denied, or controlled-failure result?
  4. Explanation: Can someone follow your configuration decisions?
  5. Boundary: Have you stated what the exercise does not demonstrate?

The output is a project summary with evidence, not simply a running installation.

Don't keep adding tools because the environment could be larger. This checkpoint should answer a specific question about your capability and give you material to discuss in an application or interview.

Begin suitable applications before the project is perfect

The calendar is a structure, not a rule that forbids applying until a particular week.

If you already have credible experience for a suitable role, apply while continuing to prepare. If your main gap is how you explain your background, work on that immediately.

Record the application version and the role so later feedback has context. Avoid treating every rejection as proof that you need another certification.

You are gathering imperfect information about fit, positioning, and readiness while building stronger evidence.

Days 31–60: Strengthen the application story

Add the next project or technical responsibility only when it addresses a relevant gap.

Revise your resume and LinkedIn positioning so the connection to identity work is visible. Label independent projects separately from employment. Practice a short walkthrough of the strongest example.

The checkpoint evidence is an application package you can defend: accurate claims, relevant examples, working project links, and a clear explanation of what you still need to learn.

The two-minute lab walkthrough is a useful test of whether your evidence is understandable rather than merely complete.

Days 61–90: Review the pattern, not just the activity count

Look at what happened across your applications and conversations.

No responses may point to targeting, eligibility, positioning, or application-channel issues. Technical interviews that repeatedly expose the same gap suggest a more specific learning task. One difficult interview is not enough to diagnose everything.

Your checkpoint evidence is a review note: what you tried, what you observed, what you can reasonably infer, and what you will change.

A plan that never changes in response to evidence is not necessarily disciplined. It may simply be ignoring information.

Keep the weekly workload small enough to repeat

Choose a cadence that fits your job and other responsibilities.

Protect time for learning, building, and applications rather than allowing study to consume the entire schedule. At the end of each week, name one thing you can now explain, demonstrate, or submit.

If the plan repeatedly fails, reduce scope or adjust the cadence before blaming motivation.
