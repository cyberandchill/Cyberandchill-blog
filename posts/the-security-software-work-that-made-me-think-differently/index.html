The Security Software Work That Made Me Think Differently About Specializing

Working with security software changed how I thought about specialization: knowing the product wasn't enough; I needed to understand the problem around it.

A feature can behave correctly while the customer still can't achieve the intended result. The cause may involve another system, an unclear requirement, or an assumption nobody tested.

That made deeper knowledge of a connected set of problems more useful to me than superficial familiarity with every interesting security tool.

A feature list doesn't explain a customer's problem

You can learn what a product advertises without understanding why a customer needs it.

The more useful questions concern the environment: what the user is trying to do, what currently prevents it, and what constraints the solution must respect.

In identity, that might involve access to an application, a difficult authentication flow, or a process that leaves the wrong permissions in place.

A specialist needs to connect the feature to the actual requirement. Otherwise, technical knowledge stays disconnected from the decision the customer is trying to make.

Depth includes knowing where the product stops

A security product rarely controls every system involved in an outcome.

An identity integration may depend on directory data, application behavior, network connectivity, or a separate administrative process. Knowing the product well includes recognizing when the evidence points outside its boundary.

That doesn't mean dismissing the problem as somebody else's responsibility. It means explaining the boundary and helping the next team investigate with useful information.

This kind of reasoning is more valuable than treating every issue as a reason to change the same familiar setting.

Use recurring questions to choose what to learn deeply

When the same type of question keeps returning, don't stop at memorizing the usual answer.

Use it to build a learning loop:

  1. Name the behavior: What is the system supposed to do?
  2. Test the explanation: Can you reproduce a small, safe example?
  3. Identify the evidence: What would distinguish this cause from another?
  4. Explain the boundary: What depends on a different tool, team, or decision?

For an IAM candidate, recurring SSO requirements in suitable jobs can point toward learning a sign-in flow and its failure points.

That doesn't mean studying every identity protocol at once. Pick a question you need to answer and go far enough to explain it reliably. Repetition becomes useful when it develops understanding, not just a quicker canned response.

Communication becomes part of the technical skill

A technically correct explanation can still be unusable if it doesn't answer the listener's question.

An administrator may need implementation detail. A business owner may need to understand the access decision. A user may need a clear description of what happens next.

Practice explaining the same scenario at those different levels without changing the facts.

A small portfolio project can help: write a short business summary, then provide the technical evidence below it. That structure makes your work easier to inspect and gives you better interview material.

Choose a specialty around work you want to repeat

A specialization isn't just a topic you're willing to study once. It is a set of problems you may spend substantial time solving.

Before committing, try a representative task. Configure a small integration, investigate a controlled failure, or model a lifecycle decision. Notice whether the reasoning interests you after the initial setup is complete.

The answer may point toward operations, engineering, governance, or a customer-facing identity role.

Use a practical test of whether identity work suits you rather than choosing only from salary claims or product popularity.

Build a career story around useful depth

My path through networking, security software, and identity is not an exact roadmap somebody else needs to copy.

The transferable lesson is to become more capable around a problem the work actually requires. Then learn to show that capability clearly and honestly.

You can start with the IT experience you already have and choose one identity gap worth closing. A focused project is often a better next move than attempting to learn an entire industry at once.
