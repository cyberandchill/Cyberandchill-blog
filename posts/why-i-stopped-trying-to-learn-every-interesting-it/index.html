Why I Stopped Trying to Learn Every Interesting IT Technology

Keep learning for curiosity separate from learning for a specific career move.

An interesting technology doesn't automatically belong in your current study plan. I can find more IT subjects appealing than I could reasonably learn well, and treating them all as priorities would keep restarting the work.

Give career learning a destination and a finish line. Save unrelated questions for later rather than abandoning a useful project whenever something new appears. You can stay curious while making measurable progress toward the responsibility you actually want next.

Interest is a good reason to explore, not always to commit

There's nothing wrong with opening a course because the subject looks useful or enjoyable.

The problem starts when an afternoon of exploration turns into a new six-month roadmap without a decision about what it replaces.

You might already be working on an identity lab, preparing an application, and studying a relevant certification. Adding another platform means less time for one of those activities.

Write that tradeoff down. “This looks interesting” is honest. “I need this for my next role” requires more evidence.

Give career learning a specific destination

A job target makes it easier to choose between competing topics.

Suppose the roles you're considering involve directory administration and application-access troubleshooting. A project that tests those responsibilities has a clearer connection than an unrelated dashboard you started because it looked impressive online.

That doesn't make the dashboard worthless. It makes it a different activity.

I prefer a plan that can explain why the next study session belongs in it. If I can't connect the topic to a requirement, a current problem, or a deliberate exploration question, it goes on a separate list.

Save new interests without letting them reset the project

Use a small parking list when a tool catches your attention. Record:

  • Tool or subject: Enough information to find it again.
  • Specific question: What you would investigate, such as whether it could help collect access-review evidence.
  • Possible relevance: Which current or future responsibility it might support.
  • Review point: When you'll decide whether to explore it further.

“Learn this whole platform” is too broad. A defined question is easier to evaluate after the initial excitement passes.

Then return to the current project. Review the list at a planned decision point instead of reopening priorities every time your feed introduces a new product.

This preserves curiosity without making it the owner of your schedule. It also shows which interests remain useful once you compare them with work you're actually trying to become capable of doing.

Finish one learning loop before opening five more

A complete loop includes understanding a problem, attempting a solution, testing it, and explaining the result.

Watching a tutorial covers only part of that loop. Installing software covers another part. Neither guarantees that you can independently describe what the setup does or diagnose a failure.

Before adding a new topic, ask what would make the current project complete enough to explain. Sometimes the missing work is only a test and a short write-up.

That final effort often creates more usable evidence than starting another environment from scratch.

Leave room for adjacent skills when they solve a real obstacle

Focus doesn't mean refusing every supporting topic. An identity project may require enough networking to understand connectivity, enough scripting to repeat a task, or enough application knowledge to interpret a sign-in problem.

Learn the supporting concept to the depth the problem requires. Then return to the main objective.

The boundary is whether the new learning helps finish the work or quietly replaces it. You can revisit deeper study later when you have a reason.

Measure the result in explanations and finished work

At the end of a month, I'd rather be able to explain one identity integration clearly than list ten technologies I briefly explored.

That preference isn't anti-learning. It's a way to make learning useful to a specific career goal.

Use a demonstration-based progress measure to check whether your study time is producing something you can show, troubleshoot, or discuss.
