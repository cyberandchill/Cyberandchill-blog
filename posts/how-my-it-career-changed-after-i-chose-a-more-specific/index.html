How My IT Career Changed After I Chose a More Specific Problem

My career became easier to explain when I could name the specific problem I helped solve, not just the field I worked in.

“Networking,” “security software,” and “identity” each contain more than anyone can learn at once. A failed authentication, incorrect application information, or obsolete access assignment gives that learning a purpose.

A narrower focus helped connect my experience to the work I wanted next. It didn't mean ignoring every supporting technology.

A broad field contains too many possible next steps

“Get better at cybersecurity” can justify almost any course.

That makes it difficult to decide what to study, what to build, and what to leave alone. Every new topic looks relevant because the goal is broad enough to include everything.

A more specific question changes the decision: “Can I explain and troubleshoot the sign-in path for this type of application?”

Now you have a reason to learn particular concepts and a way to tell whether the work helped.

A recurring problem reveals the useful supporting skills

Once you choose the problem, the supporting knowledge becomes easier to identify.

An authentication issue may require directory knowledge, protocol understanding, networking context, and application evidence. You don't need to study all those disciplines to their maximum depth before beginning.

Learn the part required to understand the current scenario, then expand when the next question justifies it.

This is different from ignoring fundamentals. It gives the fundamentals a place to attach instead of leaving them as disconnected definitions.

Depth makes the explanation more valuable

When you understand a problem beyond the first checklist, you can ask better questions and recognize misleading symptoms.

You can distinguish what the evidence proves from what remains uncertain. You can explain why a change is appropriate and what would make you reconsider it.

Those are useful capabilities in technical and customer-facing work.

They are also easier to demonstrate in an interview than a claim that you've “worked with many technologies.” A clear account of one difficult decision gives the listener something real to evaluate.

Specialization still needs a market connection

An interesting problem is not automatically the right career focus.

Check whether suitable employers need the work, whether the role fits your constraints, and whether you can realistically build the required capability.

You may enjoy a subject that belongs in your personal learning rather than your next job. That's a valid distinction.

Use current job responsibilities to test the connection. Don't treat someone else's compensation story as proof that the same path will produce the same result for you.

Try one representative problem before buying a roadmap

Choose a small scenario that lets you experience the work:

  • Directory access: Explain why one fictional account receives permission and another doesn't.
  • Application sign-in: Configure a supported integration and investigate one controlled failure.
  • Lifecycle change: Show how a fictional employee's transfer changes the access they should retain.

Finish the scenario well enough to explain its result and limitations. Then ask whether you'd like to solve more problems in that area.

This is a reversible choice of an IAM lane, not a permanent decision about your entire career. You can change direction with better information before spending heavily on training.

The test is useful even when you decide against the specialty. Learning what you don't want to do is better than collecting credentials for work you haven't examined.

Turn the focus into a clearer career story

Explain the relevant work you've already done, the specific capability you're developing, and the roles that use it.

That is a more useful story than pretending your career followed a perfect plan from the beginning. Mine did not need a perfect origin story to become more deliberate over time.

The next move can be specific even when the earlier path was not.
