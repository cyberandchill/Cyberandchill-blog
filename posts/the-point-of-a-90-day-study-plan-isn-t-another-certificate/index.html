The Point of a 90-Day Study Plan Isn't Another Certificate

At the end of a ninety-day study plan, check what you can explain, demonstrate, document, and apply for—not just which certificate you earned.

Your plan should make you more capable of the work you want. Completed videos and study hours can support that progress, but they don't establish it on their own.

Use four practical outputs to review the plan. A credential may be one of them; it shouldn't hide a weak project explanation, untested lab, or career search that never begins.

Explain a problem without hiding behind terminology

Choose a concept relevant to your target role and explain it using a concrete scenario.

For example, describe why a user can authenticate successfully but still lack permission to modify a resource. Name the identity, the action, and the decision involved.

Then handle a follow-up question. What evidence would help you distinguish a permission problem from an unavailable resource? What do you know, and what would you need to inspect?

This check reveals whether the learning has become usable reasoning or remains recognition of familiar words. You can perform it aloud or in a short written explanation.

Demonstrate a result under stated conditions

A useful project produces behavior you can test.

Define the expected outcome before configuring the environment. Record the relevant identity and action, then compare the actual result with the requirement.

Include a boundary or failure case where it matters. A configuration that allows the intended user is incomplete evidence if it also grants the same access to everybody else.

You don't need a giant environment. You need a claim, a controlled test, and a conclusion that doesn't exceed the evidence. Keep lab work clearly labeled and use fictional identities.

Document enough for somebody else to follow

Your notes may make sense to you because you remember every decision. Another reader doesn't have that context.

Write a short case study explaining the problem, your contribution, the important configuration, the tests, and the limitations. Use a diagram only where it clarifies a relationship.

Ask whether someone could identify the purpose and result without reading every command. Then ask whether a technically curious reader could find enough detail to examine the claim.

Both levels matter. A polished summary with no evidence is weak, and detailed evidence with no explanation is hard to use.

Check whether the study has strengthened your applications

Return to the openings that informed your plan and compare your candidacy before and after the work.

Ask three questions:

  • Capability: Can I now support more central responsibilities with honest examples?
  • Presentation: Does my resume explain relevant experience that was previously hard to see?
  • Direction: Did the projects confirm my target or reveal a better-fitting IAM specialty?

Those are useful career results even before an offer arrives. They don't guarantee employment, but they show whether your preparation has changed what you can credibly pursue.

Keep the answer specific. “I completed a course” is less informative than describing an access decision you can now configure, test, and explain.

Start suitable applications when the fit is plausible. The end of ninety days is a review point, not an artificial rule preventing you from speaking with employers earlier.

Review the weakest output each week

You may discover that you're building faster than you can explain, or studying more than you're demonstrating. Another week may show that the technical work is solid but the application materials are still vague.

Use that imbalance to choose the next action. More hours in the strongest category won't automatically fix the weakest one.

Keep the review brief. A sentence under each output is enough to see whether the plan is producing something useful.
