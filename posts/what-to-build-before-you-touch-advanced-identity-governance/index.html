What to Build Before You Touch Advanced Identity Governance Tools

Before learning an advanced identity governance platform, model one access decision without it.

Use fictional employees, a few applications, and a clear approval process. Show who needs access, who can approve it, how it changes, and how you check the result.

This won't prove you can administer the platform. It will help you understand what its workflows and integrations are supposed to accomplish, instead of learning buttons without understanding the decisions behind them.

Create a small identity and access dataset

Start with a handful of fictional employees and contractors.

For each person, record a role, manager, department, employment status, and any relevant end date. Then list the applications or resources and the access each person currently has.

Include a few deliberate problems: an old departmental assignment, a contractor past the intended end date, and a permission with no clear owner.

Keep the dataset small enough that you can explain every row. More records don't automatically make the exercise more realistic or useful.

Define who can make each decision

Identify the resource owner, the person who can approve access, and the person responsible for implementing the change.

Those roles may overlap in a small organization, but don't assume they are interchangeable.

Write down what happens when the expected owner is unavailable or the request lacks enough information. An unresolved decision should remain visible rather than being silently converted into approval.

This reveals the governance questions that a tool will eventually need to support.

Model one request from beginning to end

Choose a fictional employee who needs a specific application role.

Capture the business reason, requested permission, approval, implementation, and verification. If any step is simulated, label it.

Then introduce an exception, such as temporary access for a short project. Define the end date and the person responsible for checking it.

The exercise should show how the decision changes the access state, not merely how a form moves through several boxes.

Add a review and a removal

Now ask whether the access remains justified after a role change or project completion.

Record the review decision and the resulting action. Check the before-and-after state where your lab supports it, and document any application behavior outside the scope.

This connects governance to operations. A decision marked complete is not enough if the permission remains unchanged.

Use the purpose of an access review to explain what the reviewer needs to know and what happens after the decision.

List the data and decisions an automated workflow would need

Once the manual process works, examine what automation would require.

Question Why it matters
Where does identity information come from? The workflow needs a reliable input.
How are application accounts and permissions represented? A decision must map to an actual change.
What happens when approval is missing? Silence needs an explicit handling rule.
How are failed changes detected? Approval alone doesn't prove implementation.

You don't need to implement every integration in the first project. Record what would be required and what your model leaves untested.

A worksheet can demonstrate process reasoning, ownership, and exception handling. It cannot establish hands-on expertise in a governance product you haven't used. Keep that boundary visible when you describe the project to employers.

Choose platform learning from the gap you discovered

Your next step might be understanding application provisioning, learning a review feature, or practicing how a workflow handles exceptions.

That is a clearer starting point than opening a large product and trying to learn every screen at once.

Check current product documentation and licensing before selecting a practice environment. Tool availability changes, while the need to understand decisions and evidence remains.
