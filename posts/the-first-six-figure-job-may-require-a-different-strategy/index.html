The First Six-Figure Job May Require a Different Strategy Than Your First IT Job

Your next IT role may require more than the evidence that helped you get your first one. Employers may need you to own a defined responsibility, investigate unfamiliar failures, or explain the impact of a change.

Study that change in expectations rather than treating six figures as a universal skill threshold.

Use a suitable job posting to identify what the employer needs handled, then show where your existing work supports the claim and where you still need practical development.

Stop presenting every achievement as beginner potential

A mid-career resume can accidentally read like an entry-level application.

It may emphasize enthusiasm, foundational certificates, and a long tools list while giving little space to the work the person has actually handled.

Look for examples of responsibility: a difficult investigation, a safe change, a process you improved, or a recurring task you became reliable at completing.

Do not invent leadership or business outcomes. Describe the judgment and ownership that genuinely existed inside your role.

Turn the role's responsibilities into questions about capability

Choose a realistic target role and translate its main duties:

Responsibility Question your evidence should answer
Investigate access failures Can you narrow the cause using evidence rather than guesses?
Maintain identity lifecycle processes Can you explain and verify the relevant account and access changes?
Support proposed changes Can you identify the scope, impact, and validation needed?
Work across teams Can you give an application owner a clear, useful escalation?

These questions give you a better preparation target than “become a six-figure candidate.”

Compensation structures and job expectations differ. The salary number doesn't establish one standard everyone must meet.

Use the actual opportunity to decide which examples, projects, and interview explanations matter. Then prepare those specifically instead of repeating the broad entry-level strategy that got you started.

Use your existing work before adding another credential

For each question, look for a professional example you can explain accurately.

You may discover that the gap is presentation rather than knowledge. “Handled user tickets” can hide substantial troubleshooting or careful access work.

You may also discover a genuine capability gap. That is useful information, not a reason to dismiss your entire background.

The difference between transferable experience and remaining skill gaps helps you build a plan that recognizes both rather than pretending one cancels the other.

Build evidence for the missing responsibility

Choose an independent project that tests the specific gap.

If the role involves SSO troubleshooting, a controlled integration failure may be more relevant than installing another unrelated dashboard. If it involves lifecycle operations, demonstrate a change in access and verify the result.

Label the project as a lab and describe its limitations. It can show understanding and preparation without pretending to replace production experience.

The purpose is to strengthen the case for the next responsibility, not to accumulate projects until the application looks busy.

Practice explaining decisions, not just steps

An interviewer may ask why you chose a configuration, what else you considered, and how you knew the result was correct.

Prepare to answer from your actual work or lab evidence. Explain the requirement, the relevant constraints, the action you took, and the checks you performed.

When you do not know, say what you would verify and where the uncertainty lies. A careful boundary is more credible than a confident answer assembled from unfamiliar terminology.

This is a different preparation task from memorizing definitions for a foundational exam.

Evaluate the offer as a job, not a milestone trophy

Reaching a compensation target can matter, but examine the full arrangement: guaranteed pay, variable pay, responsibility, availability, travel, and the work you will develop next.

A number alone cannot tell you whether the move improves your situation.
