What I Got Wrong About the Path From IT Support to Higher Pay

The career mistake I'd warn a support professional about is assuming that doing more of the current job automatically prepares you for the next one.

Experience and effort matter, but the next employer needs particular responsibilities performed. My path through networking, security software, and identity makes more sense when I look at what changed in the work, not only the years that passed.

Use that distinction to decide what your next move needs to add.

More experience is useful when the capability changes

Another year can deepen your judgment, broaden your responsibility, or give you stronger examples of difficult work.

It can also repeat the same tasks without changing what you can independently explain or own.

Neither outcome is determined by the calendar. Look at the work.

Ask what you can now investigate, configure, communicate, or decide that you couldn't handle before. That answer is more useful for career planning than the anniversary date alone.

A higher-paying direction still needs a specific target

“Move up in IT” leaves too many decisions unresolved.

A role with identity administration, integration, or governance responsibilities gives you a clearer comparison. You can identify relevant experience and the missing capability instead of assuming another broad qualification will solve the whole problem.

The target doesn't need to remain fixed forever. It needs to be concrete enough to guide the next project and application.

Without it, even disciplined study can stay disconnected from the work you hope to be paid to do.

Positioning is part of the work, not a cosmetic afterthought

A future employer may not recognize the identity relevance inside a support title.

You need to explain the account, access, authentication, lifecycle, or troubleshooting responsibilities you actually performed. That doesn't require changing the title or exaggerating ownership.

It requires choosing examples that answer the employer's question about capability.

A stronger resume can't create experience you don't have. It can stop hiding experience that is already relevant.

Learning needs to produce something you can use

A course can help you understand a topic, but the career benefit is harder to evaluate when the learning never becomes a demonstration, application, or better work example.

Build a small project and explain the result. Use a real role requirement to decide what the project should show.

Then put the evidence into the job-search process rather than waiting for a perfect portfolio.

Use early application feedback in a study plan to avoid spending months preparing for a role you haven't tested against the market.

Evaluate the life attached to the higher salary

The next position may change more than your income.

Before deciding, compare:

  • Schedule: Expected hours and interruptions outside them.
  • Location: Where you're actually allowed to work.
  • Travel: Frequency, notice, and how it affects your commitments.
  • Support responsibility: What you own when systems fail.
  • Working environment: Whether the team's expectations fit how you want to work.

A larger salary can be worthwhile. It shouldn't hide a role that conflicts with your priorities, particularly when flexibility is part of why you chose IT.

You are agreeing to perform a specific job under specific conditions. Treat the number as one part of that agreement, not a milestone that automatically makes every other tradeoff acceptable. A good career move needs to make sense after the offer excitement wears off.

Make the next move smaller and more concrete

Find one suitable role, identify the evidence you already have, and choose one gap worth closing.

That is a manageable plan even when the larger ambition feels far away. It also gives you a way to revise the direction based on results instead of repeating the same preparation indefinitely.

The lesson is not that support experience lacks value. It's that you need to decide which part of it supports the next responsibility and build the missing evidence on purpose.
