Why I'm More Interested in Useful Skills Than Impressive Titles

A title gets my attention; an explanation of useful work tells me whether the role or skill is worth pursuing.

After working across network support, security software, and identity, I'm more interested in what a position lets me become capable of doing than how impressive it sounds on a profile.

Titles still matter as signals. They just don't tell you enough about responsibility, independence, or the problems you'll learn to handle.

Ask what problem the person can take to a checked result

“Identity engineer” can describe very different work in two organizations.

Compare the actual responsibility:

Same broad title, different work What to clarify
Application integration Who designs, configures, tests, and supports the connection?
Access administration Who approves the decision and who implements it?
Authentication troubleshooting Who gathers evidence, chooses a correction, and verifies recovery?

All can be valuable. The title alone doesn't reveal the depth of experience involved.

A better question is: What problem can this person take from an unclear request to a verified result?

Use that question on your own career options. It points toward a responsibility to develop instead of another label to chase. It also makes your existing work easier to describe accurately without overstating independence or ownership.

Look for judgment inside ordinary tasks

Routine work can contain useful technical judgment. An access request may require checking who approved it, identifying the right group, understanding what that group permits, and confirming the intended result. A login ticket may require separating a user-account problem from an application problem.

The task doesn't need a dramatic name to matter.

The important distinction is whether you understand the work or only know which button normally comes next. When the normal procedure fails, can you explain what you would investigate and why?

Choose skills that connect to work you actually want

A skill can be interesting without being the right next investment for you.

Suppose you're targeting identity administration. Learning how account lifecycle changes affect access may help you more immediately than collecting an unrelated technology badge because the subject looks advanced.

That doesn't make the other subject worthless. It means your time has a purpose.

Use suitable job postings to check the connection. Look for recurring responsibilities, then decide which one you could become better at demonstrating. Keep working conditions in the comparison, not just technical requirements.

Keep credentials and titles in their proper place

Credentials can help communicate learning or satisfy an explicit requirement. Titles can help people understand where you've worked.

Neither should have to carry the entire story.

If your application depends on a hiring team assuming that a title proves competence, you haven't given them enough evidence. Add a relevant example, project, or explanation of responsibility.

The same applies when evaluating an opportunity. Ask about the actual work, the decisions you'll own, and the support available. A larger title without a workable role can be a poor trade.

Test a skill with a small, complete example

Take one identity problem and explain it from beginning to end.

For a fictional employee changing teams, describe the access they need, the approval, the change, the old access that must be removed, and the tests that would confirm the result.

You don't need to build every enterprise system to make that explanation useful. You do need to be clear about what your example demonstrates and what it doesn't.

That is also why owning an outcome is different from knowing a tool. The tool is part of the work, not the entire value of it.

Make your next career decision about capability

Before enrolling in another course or applying for a more impressive title, finish this sentence:

“This move should help me become capable of…”

If the answer stays vague, investigate further. If it describes a responsibility you want and a credible way to develop it, you have a stronger basis for the decision.

I wrote The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook around that approach: choose a role, identify the relevant gaps, build practical proof, and use it in a career move. It's a $9 guide, not a promise that a new title automatically brings a particular salary.

I'd rather be able to explain the value of my work than depend on someone being impressed by the name of my job.
