You Don't Need a Perfect Roadmap. You Need Next Friday's Milestone.

Choose a concrete milestone for next Friday before refining a six-month career roadmap.

The milestone should produce evidence or information that changes your next decision. That could be a completed access test, a clear explanation of one concept, or an application to a suitable role.

A detailed plan is useful only when it helps you act. If you still spend every study session deciding what to open, narrow the immediate deliverable and define what counts as finished. Then use the result to improve the longer plan.

Write a deliverable, not a topic

“Study identity governance” is a topic. “Create a fictional access-review record with an owner, a decision, and a verified change” is a deliverable.

The second statement tells you what to produce and how to recognize progress.

Keep the scope small enough for the time you actually have. A milestone that requires an unfamiliar platform, a new environment, and several integrations is probably several milestones disguised as one.

A realistic finish line is not a lack of ambition. It is a way to get feedback sooner.

Connect the milestone to one uncertainty

Perhaps you do not know whether your support experience is relevant to an IAM role. The milestone could be a requirement-to-experience map for one suitable posting.

Perhaps you understand a protocol definition but not the sign-in sequence. The milestone could be a diagram of a tested application flow.

Perhaps you keep delaying applications. The milestone could be one tailored application supported by your current evidence.

The point is to choose an action that answers the question keeping you stuck, rather than selecting whichever task feels easiest.

Define the evidence that would count as finished

For a lab, that might include the intended behavior, a comparison test, and a short explanation of the result.

For an application, it might include a clear fit statement, relevant resume examples, and a record of submission.

For research, it might include a comparison of responsibilities across several roles and a decision about which one to investigate further.

Avoid a finish line based only on time spent. Two hours of reading can be useful, but the milestone should explain what the reading enabled you to understand or decide.

Decide what to leave out this week

Write a short exclusion list. You might postpone automation, a second application, a new certification, or polishing the portfolio design.

Those ideas can remain valuable. They simply do not belong inside the current milestone unless they are necessary to answer the chosen question.

Choosing one IAM skill for a two-week focus is a useful next step when every related topic starts competing for attention.

A focused week should not require you to stop being curious. It requires a place to park curiosity that would derail the current work.

Use the Friday review to choose the next useful task

Inspect the result with three questions:

  1. Completion: Did I produce the deliverable I defined?
  2. Learning: What did it reveal about my capability or the role?
  3. Uncertainty: What still needs an answer before the next decision?

Then change the plan when the evidence calls for it:

  • If the milestone was too large, reduce the next one.
  • If the project exposed a fundamental gap, address that concept.
  • If you already have enough evidence for a suitable application, apply instead of adding preparation automatically.

This review prevents milestones from becoming another activity counter. A completed checkbox matters when it improves your understanding or your next move.

Keep a short note of the decision so the following week begins with a task, not another round of choosing resources.

Use a broader plan as a guide, not a prerequisite

A 90-day structure can help you keep role selection, learning, projects, and applications connected. You do not need every detail settled before starting the first useful week.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook provides that structure for $9. Its value is in helping you choose and sequence useful work, not in making the roadmap look impressive.

By next Friday, aim to have something you can explain, demonstrate, or decide that you could not at the start of the week.
