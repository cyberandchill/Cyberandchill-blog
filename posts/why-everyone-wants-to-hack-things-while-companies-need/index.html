Why Everyone Wants to Hack Things While Companies Need People to Manage Access

Cybersecurity includes more than finding ways into systems. It also includes deciding who should have access, removing permissions people no longer need, and keeping those controls working.

IAM is worth comparing with offensive security before you commit to a career path. The better choice depends on the problems you enjoy solving and the experience you can build on.

A dramatic demonstration might attract your attention. A job description—and a small sample of the actual work—will tell you more about whether you'd enjoy the role.

Picture the ordinary access problem

Take a fictional employee who changed teams six months ago.

They still belong to a group from the old role. Nobody has confirmed whether that access is still appropriate.

You don't need a dramatic exploit to see the issue. A permission can remain in place after its business justification disappears.

The work now involves identifying the owner, establishing what access is required, coordinating a change, and confirming the result.

It may include technical investigation. It also includes asking a person to make a decision they have been putting off.

That combination is part of the job.

Offensive and identity work can strengthen each other

A penetration test can reveal a weakness. An identity team may help change the account, permission, or process that allowed it.

The two types of work are not opposing career philosophies.

They can simply emphasize different parts of the same security problem. One may focus on demonstrating an attack path. Another may focus on operating controls, maintaining access, and ensuring changes continue to work.

Avoid choosing a career by insulting the alternative.

The useful question is which responsibilities fit your interests, current experience, and willingness to keep learning.

You can respect a field without making it your own next move.

Ask whether you enjoy ownership after the finding

Some people love the investigation that proves something is possible. Others are more interested in fixing the process and making sure the fix survives ordinary business changes.

Identity work can put you close to that second responsibility.

For example, an access review needs more than a spreadsheet of accounts. Someone must decide what access is justified, handle exceptions, and ensure the agreed changes actually occur.

Microsoft's identity operations guidance includes lifecycle and assignment management, which illustrates the ongoing nature of the work. Source: Microsoft IAM operations guide.

A person who enjoys making systems understandable and maintainable may find that satisfying.

A person who hates coordination should investigate the role carefully rather than assuming it is all technical configuration.

Compare two small projects before choosing a specialty

Try evaluating these two project briefs rather than choosing whichever job title sounds more exciting:

Project Work you would practice
Authorized security testing Investigate a permitted test environment and explain a weakness you found
Identity access management Define which fictional users need a resource, implement permissions, and verify changes after a transfer

Both require careful reasoning and useful documentation. Neither becomes impressive just because you install the tools associated with it.

After trying the work, ask yourself:

  • Which questions did you genuinely want to investigate further?
  • Would you tolerate the work when the documentation was incomplete?
  • Did you prefer finding the weakness or maintaining the controls afterward?

Your answers are more relevant to a career decision than which topic gets the strongest reaction online.

Choose from the work backward

Read actual job responsibilities before buying a path.

For IAM, look at whether a role is mostly administration, integration, governance, privileged access, or a mix. For offensive work, inspect the type of testing and reporting expected.

Then compare the role with your current evidence.

You may already have a more direct starting point in one area. That is worth considering, but it shouldn't force you into work you dislike.

Also resist promises that one specialty guarantees an easy schedule or immediate six-figure pay. The employer and responsibilities matter.
