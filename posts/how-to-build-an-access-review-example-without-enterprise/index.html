How to Build an Access Review Example Without Enterprise Software

You can learn access-review reasoning without buying an enterprise governance platform.

Use fictional records to show how an owner decides whether access remains appropriate and how that decision becomes a checked action.

This is a process prototype, not a replacement for a production governance system. Keep the scope clear while modeling the information, responsibility, exceptions, and follow-through that make the review useful beyond selecting a checkbox.

Create a fictional roster and access list

Use a handful of sample people, one sample application, and a few access levels.

Record the person's current responsibility, the access they hold, the resource owner, and any temporary end condition in the scenario. Keep the dataset small enough to review manually.

Include a few deliberate cases: a person whose access still fits, a person who changed roles, and a contractor whose project ended.

The examples should make the decisions meaningful without using real employee information or copying an employer's access records.

Define what the reviewer needs to decide

The question is not simply whether the person recognizes the user's name.

The reviewer should determine whether the current access matches the person's current approved need. That may result in retaining, modifying, removing, or investigating the access further.

Provide enough context for the decision. An unexplained list of account identifiers and group names makes the exercise about guessing. A useful review record connects the identity, resource, permission, and business responsibility.

Assign the decision to a responsible owner

In the fictional process, identify who can approve continued access and who implements changes.

Those do not have to be the same role. The person operating the technical system may not be the person who knows whether the business need remains valid.

Microsoft's access-review overview illustrates this broader governance function in a platform context.

Your manual model should preserve the important responsibilities even though it does not reproduce the platform's automation, integrations, or reporting capabilities.

Record a decision that another reviewer can understand

For each sample access record, capture:

  • Outcome: Retain, remove, change, or follow up.
  • Reason: The business context supporting that outcome.
  • Owner: Who can make or clarify the decision.
  • Next action: What implementation or further information is required.

For example: “Remove the old project role because the fictional employee transferred teams and the resource owner confirmed there is no continuing need.”

If context is missing, record the uncertainty and assign a follow-up rather than guessing. A review should not turn incomplete information into false confidence.

The record needs to preserve reasoning, not merely completion. Someone examining the prototype later should be able to understand why the access decision was appropriate and what still needed to happen before the review could be considered complete.

Demonstrate implementation and verification

A review is not complete simply because the owner selected “remove.”

Show how the decision would be passed to the responsible implementer and how the resulting state would be checked. If you have a suitable isolated lab, carry out the approved sample change and test the relevant access.

If you are only modeling the process, label the implementation step as proposed rather than completed.

Use a finding-to-correction lab structure to make the distinction between a decision, a change, and a verified result clear.

Publish the prototype with its limits

The portfolio can include the fictional dataset, review instructions, sample decisions, action record, and test evidence where implemented.

Explain what an enterprise system would need beyond the prototype, such as integrations, scalable workflow, appropriate auditability, and operational controls. Avoid claiming compliance or production readiness from a small manual exercise.

The useful evidence is that you understand the governance problem before choosing a tool.
