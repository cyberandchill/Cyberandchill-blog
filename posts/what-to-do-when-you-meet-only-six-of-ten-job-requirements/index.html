What to Do When You Meet Only Six of Ten Job Requirements

Meeting six of ten requirements doesn't tell you whether to apply for an IAM job. The importance of the four missing requirements matters more than the percentage.

A location or authorization constraint may rule out the role. An unfamiliar tool may be learnable alongside work you already understand. Missing the central responsibility is a different issue.

Evaluate each gap, then decide whether your existing evidence supports a plausible transition. You don't need perfect confidence, but you do need an honest explanation of where you'd contribute and where you'd need support.

Separate genuine gates from preferences

Start with requirements that determine whether the opportunity is workable.

Work authorization, required clearance, location, schedule, and explicitly mandatory credentials can matter independently of your technical potential.

Don't ignore them because somebody told you nobody meets every requirement.

Read the posting carefully and resolve ambiguity through an appropriate recruiter or employer contact when available.

A role that cannot accommodate your circumstances is different from a role where you lack experience with one product.

Treating both as “four missing bullets” hides the important distinction.

Identify the job's central responsibility

Look for the work the employer is actually hiring someone to own.

If the role centers on integrating identity systems and you have never configured or troubleshot an integration, that may be a substantial gap.

If you've performed similar responsibilities on another platform but haven't used this employer's exact tool, the gap may be narrower.

You still need to learn the differences. Transferability isn't automatic.

Write a paragraph explaining the role's main purpose in plain language. Then ask what evidence you have that you can contribute to it.

That is more useful than calculating a match percentage.

Attach a real example to each requirement you meet

For each relevant responsibility, choose evidence you can explain:

  • An investigation: An account issue you examined and the checks you performed.
  • An operational task: An approved access workflow you followed and verified.
  • An implementation: A configuration you personally applied, with its scope clear.
  • A lab: A test you designed or completed in a clearly labeled learning environment.

Keep professional and lab experience separate. Both can help explain your capability, but they demonstrate different conditions and levels of responsibility.

Write down your contribution rather than listing only the product. “Used Entra” leaves the interviewer guessing; a precise example shows what you actually did with it.

That evidence helps an employer judge where you could be productive and what support you might need. It also prevents a superficially matching keyword list from hiding a genuine technical gap.

Choose which gaps to address before applying

Some gaps can be investigated quickly enough to improve your application.

You might read the relevant product documentation, complete a small test, or learn the terminology needed to describe a system accurately.

Other gaps represent months or years of responsibility. A weekend project will not convert into production ownership.

Don't delay every application until you've completed an imaginary perfect profile. But don't use a lab as a way to conceal a genuine experience requirement either.

A reasonable decision might be to apply now with transparent limits while developing one relevant skill.

Another might be to target a role with a more suitable level of ownership first.

Explain the transition without apologizing for it

You don't need to begin an application by listing everything you lack.

Lead with the relevant capability you have. For example, you might explain your experience with access operations and troubleshooting, then describe the project you've built to deepen your understanding of application sign-in.

Keep the explanation tied to the role. Avoid claiming that you “learn anything instantly.” A specific example of learning and applying something is more informative than a promise about your potential.

Also be ready to discuss what you would need to learn during onboarding.

Use the response as information, not a verdict

A rejection doesn't identify its own cause. The role could have changed, another applicant could fit better, or your application might not have communicated the relevant evidence. Technical readiness is only one possible explanation.

Look for patterns across a sensible set of opportunities rather than drawing a major conclusion from one response.

When feedback is available, use it to refine the target or the next learning task.
