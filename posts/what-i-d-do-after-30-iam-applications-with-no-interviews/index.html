What I'd Do After 30 IAM Applications With No Interviews

After 30 IAM applications with no interviews, audit the fit, resume, and application process before buying another certification.

Silence at the first stage doesn't tell you which technical skill is missing. The roles may have been unsuitable, the relevant experience may be hard to see, or the application route may not have worked as expected.

Treat the submissions as records to examine. They are useful feedback about your search, not proof that you cannot move into identity work.

Separate close-fit applications from long shots

Review each application against the same questions:

  1. Did the location and work-authorization conditions fit?
  2. Could you meet the schedule and other mandatory requirements?
  3. Was the required experience reasonably connected to your background?
  4. Were the core responsibilities a plausible next step?
  5. Was the position accepting applications through the channel you used?

Keep ambitious applications in the record, but label them separately from close matches. A senior architecture opening and a suitable identity operations role don't test the same thing about your candidacy.

You may discover that 30 submissions contained only a handful of comparable opportunities.

That changes the next action. Instead of assuming your whole career story failed, first improve the set of jobs against which you're testing it. Then examine how clearly your application shows the relevant experience.

Compare the resume with the main responsibilities

Pick three of the closest-fit postings and read your submitted resume beside them.

Can a reader quickly find evidence for the main responsibilities? Or does the document lead with unrelated tools, broad claims, and early-career details?

You are not trying to copy the posting word for word. You are checking whether your genuine experience is visible in terms relevant to the role.

The process for choosing which resume bullets belong in an IAM application can help remove material that obscures the strongest evidence.

Inspect the basic application path

Check that the contact information is correct, the resume file opens, the formatting remains readable, and the project links work while signed out.

Confirm that required fields were completed accurately. Keep a record of the version you submitted so you are not reviewing a better draft than the employer received.

These checks may sound unglamorous, but they are worth completing before concluding that the problem is advanced technical knowledge.

Do not invent explanations about automated filters without evidence. Fix what you can directly inspect first.

Ask someone to evaluate the match, not your worth

A useful review question is: “Based on this posting and this resume, what relevant responsibility would you expect me to handle, and what is unclear?”

That produces more actionable feedback than asking whether the resume is good.

Seek someone who understands the work or hiring context when possible. Their view is still one perspective, not a definitive verdict.

Look for concrete confusion: unclear scope, unsupported claims, missing examples, or a mismatch between the role and the experience presented.

Change one major variable for the next suitable group

Choose a revision based on the audit. You might narrow the role target, rewrite the top section around relevant responsibilities, or improve the project evidence. Keep other major elements stable enough that you can understand what changed.

Record outcomes without pretending a small sample proves causation. Hiring timing, competition, and employer decisions remain outside your control.

The aim is to learn more from the next applications than you learned from sending another identical set.

Study more only when the evidence points to a learning gap

If the audit shows that you lack a central responsibility across otherwise suitable roles, choose a focused project or learning task.

Do not use additional study as an automatic response to uncomfortable uncertainty. Sometimes the next improvement belongs in targeting or explanation rather than technical preparation.
