The Difference Between Authentication and Authorization Gets People Hired

Authentication and authorization are useful interview concepts when you can apply them to a real access problem.

A person can sign in successfully and still be correctly denied access to a resource. Knowing that helps you choose evidence and avoid fixing the wrong thing.

Consider an employee who can authenticate but cannot open a finance report. Before changing a password or adding a group, establish whether the report should be available to that employee. The distinction alone won't get you hired; the reasoning it supports can strengthen your technical answer.

Authentication establishes the identity being presented

The sign-in process gives the system a basis for accepting who is making the request. Depending on the environment, that may involve passwords, additional factors, certificates, or other supported methods.

A successful authentication result doesn't tell you that the person should receive every resource available in the environment.

In the example, the employee can complete sign-in. That narrows the investigation, but it doesn't prove that every part of the access path is correct.

Be careful about describing “login worked” as a complete diagnosis.

Authorization evaluates the requested action

The resource must decide whether that identity can perform the requested operation.

Reading a report, modifying it, exporting data, and administering other users are different actions. A person may be allowed one and denied another.

That is why “has access” can be an unhelpful description. Ask access to what, for which action, under which conditions.

Confirm whether the denial matches the intended requirement

Suppose the employee belongs to a department that should not read the finance report. The denial may be the correct result.

Check three questions before making a change:

  1. Intended access: What should this person be allowed to do?
  2. Decision owner: Has an authorized owner approved the requested permission?
  3. Observed boundary: Does the denial match that requirement, or contradict it?

Resetting credentials won't resolve an authorization decision. Granting broad group membership just to remove the error could violate the intended access model.

Sometimes the appropriate outcome is confirming the control works and explaining the approved request process.

That is still troubleshooting: you have clarified the symptom, verified the expected behavior, and avoided an unnecessary or inappropriate change. The aim isn't to make every screen show success regardless of the requirement.

An unexpected denial needs more specific evidence

Now suppose the employee does have an approved need.

Check the identity used, the requested resource, the assigned role or group, and the relevant session or application state. Compare the observed result with a known-good test where appropriate.

Don't assume the application reads the same permission source you changed. A directory group, application assignment, and local application role may be separate parts of the path.

The distinction helps you choose evidence instead of repeating a password reset that cannot address the actual permission problem.

Demonstrate the difference in a small lab

Create two fictional users with different access requirements.

Verify that both can authenticate in the intended environment. Then show that one can read the protected resource while the other receives the expected denial.

Change only an approved test permission, refresh the relevant user context as required, and observe the result. Record what changed and what did not.

The demonstration is stronger when it includes both allowed and denied actions. It gives you a concrete example to support the definitions.

Turn the distinction into an interview explanation

A clear answer could say: “I would first confirm whether the user authenticated successfully, then identify the specific action being denied and compare it with the intended authorization.”

Follow with an actual work example or a clearly labeled lab. Explain the boundary of your responsibility and the evidence you used.
