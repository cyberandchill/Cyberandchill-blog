The Certification Decisions I'd Reconsider If I Started Over

If I started again, I'd ask what responsibility or opportunity each certification was meant to help me reach before paying for it.

That doesn't mean the learning behind my earlier choices was wasted. It means I would make the connection between study, practical ability, and job requirements clearer.

Hindsight is useful for improving the next decision. It shouldn't turn a real, uneven career path into a perfect formula someone else is expected to copy.

Separate studying a subject from purchasing the exam

A topic can be worth learning even when the credential isn't your immediate priority.

I'd consider the two decisions separately:

Decision Question I'd ask
Study the material Which concepts do I need to understand or apply?
Build evidence What could I demonstrate after learning them?
Take the exam Does assessment or credential recognition add something important?

An explicit employer requirement can make the exam worthwhile. So can a structured assessment that genuinely serves the plan. Neither is the same as buying it because it follows the last badge in a familiar sequence.

I would identify the concepts and evidence first, then decide whether the exam belongs. That keeps curiosity, capability, and credentialing connected without pretending they are identical investments.

I would check the target role earlier

It is easy to study a respected certification and assume the job choice will become obvious afterward.

A more deliberate approach starts with several suitable roles and compares their responsibilities. That reveals which knowledge is foundational, which is specialized, and which might be unrelated to the next move.

The target can change as you learn. It still gives the first decision a reason.

I would rather revise a role-informed plan than spend months following a sequence with no clear destination.

I would pair more study with a practical explanation

After learning a concept, I would ask how to demonstrate or explain it through a small scenario.

For identity, that could mean testing an access boundary, tracing a sign-in, or documenting an employee's access change. The project doesn't need to reproduce a large company.

The purpose is to discover what I understand beyond the definition.

A certification can provide structure. A practical example can reveal which parts of that structure I can actually use and which still need work.

I would be more deliberate about renewal

An existing credential creates a new decision when maintenance or renewal is required.

The answer should depend on current employer requirements, the roles I'm pursuing, and the knowledge I still use—not only the fact that I spent time earning it before.

I would check the current rules and consequences carefully before allowing anything required to lapse.

The point is not to recommend letting credentials expire. It is to make maintenance a conscious choice connected to the work and opportunities that matter now.

I would avoid treating other people's results as a prediction

A certification may appear in someone's successful career story without being the only reason for the outcome.

Their prior experience, location, timing, responsibilities, and application strategy may differ substantially from yours.

Use the story to discover a possibility, then investigate whether the same credential fits your own gap.

See why certification salary surveys don't predict your salary before turning a reported number into a purchase decision.

I would move from preparation to applications sooner where appropriate

There can be a point where another credential adds less than using the evidence you already have.

If a suitable role is within reach, an accurate application and a technical conversation can reveal what matters next. You can keep learning while that process happens.

You don't need to finish every imagined prerequisite before testing your positioning.

The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook brings these decisions together: choose a role, identify gaps, build three practical lab types, and work a 90-day plan. The $9 guide is the structure I would want around the certification choice, not a promise that one exam produces a particular salary.

The lesson is not to erase the path you took. It's to give the next credential a clearer job to do.
