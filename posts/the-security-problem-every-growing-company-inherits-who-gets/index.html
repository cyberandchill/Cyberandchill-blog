The Security Problem Every Growing Company Inherits: Who Gets Access?

As a company grows, access decisions become harder to trace through memory and informal messages.

The problem isn't only more accounts. It's losing track of why each permission exists and who can decide whether it still belongs. New applications, contractors, and team changes add more relationships to maintain.

Start with ownership and understandable access patterns before adding complexity. That gives the technical work a business purpose and helps you identify whether administration, lifecycle work, or governance is the identity responsibility you want to develop.

Growth creates more ways for access to become unclear

Imagine a fictional company that adds a sales team, an external accountant, and several contractors.

Each addition creates reasonable requests. Sales needs customer records. The accountant needs financial information. Contractors need project materials.

Now imagine that people change teams, a contractor's project ends, and the original application owner leaves.

The access that made sense when it was granted may no longer make sense. Without a repeatable process, nobody has a reliable view of what should change.

Assign ownership before buying complexity

Start by identifying who owns the decision for each important resource.

The identity team may operate the access mechanism, but the business owner usually has information about the work the access supports. Technical administrators shouldn't have to guess whether a person still needs a financial role.

Write down the owner, the types of access available, and the route for approval. Where ownership is unclear, treat that as an unresolved issue rather than filling the gap with a broad default permission.

A new tool cannot supply a business decision that nobody has agreed to make.

Define a small number of understandable access patterns

Look for repeated needs. People performing the same function may require a common set of permissions, while some responsibilities need separate approval.

The goal is not to force every person into an enormous role containing everything they might ever need. It is to make common access easier to understand and exceptions easier to identify.

For the fictional sales team, basic customer-record access might be standard. Exporting all customer information could require a separate decision.

That distinction makes later reviews more useful than asking whether someone needs “sales access.”

Review the old access whenever someone changes roles

For a department transfer, record the intended before-and-after state:

  • Retain: Which existing permissions remain justified?
  • Remove: Which permissions belonged to work the employee no longer performs?
  • Add: Which new resources are approved for the new role?
  • Time-limit: Is overlap needed, and who will check that the exception ends?

Without this comparison, access can accumulate through a series of individually approved requests. Each change may look reasonable while the combined set no longer matches the person's job.

Handling access when someone changes teams is therefore a review of existing entitlements as well as fulfillment of the new request.

Document the decision owner and reason. A completed onboarding process doesn't prevent later drift, and a successful new assignment doesn't explain why old access should continue indefinitely.

Make removal and review observable

A process should show more than the request to remove access. It should show whether the relevant change actually occurred.

For a small lab, you can model this with a list of assignments, owners, decisions, and test results. Include one stale contractor and one employee with an old departmental permission.

Record who decided the access should end, what changed, and how you verified the new state. Keep the dataset fictional and state which steps are manual.

The exercise demonstrates governance reasoning without pretending you deployed a complete enterprise platform.

Use the business problem to choose your IAM direction

This kind of work can involve directory administration, application integrations, governance, and privileged access. You don't have to master every area before choosing an entry point.

Notice which part you enjoy: troubleshooting the mechanism, designing the workflow, reviewing the decisions, or explaining the risk to an owner.
