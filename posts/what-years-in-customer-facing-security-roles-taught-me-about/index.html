What Years in Customer-Facing Security Roles Taught Me About Hiring Value

Customer-facing security work taught me that knowing a feature and helping someone use it responsibly are different skills.

The other person needs to know whether the system fits the problem, where its limits are, and what to investigate when the expected result doesn't appear.

That changes how I evaluate hiring value, too. Technical usefulness includes the explanation, evidence, and follow-through—not only familiarity with the product or confidence during a demonstration.

Start with the problem the other person is trying to solve

A technical explanation is easier to follow when it begins with the intended outcome.

For identity work, that may be an employee reaching an application, a team receiving appropriate access, or an organization changing how authentication is handled.

If you begin with every feature you know, the listener has to decide which details matter.

I prefer the reverse order: establish the problem, identify the relevant requirement, and then explain the technology that addresses it. That makes technical depth useful rather than merely visible.

Be clear about what the system does not do

Credibility depends partly on resisting the urge to make a capability sound broader than it is.

A feature may work under specific conditions. An integration may depend on another system. A successful test may cover one scenario rather than every possible deployment.

Those limits should be part of the explanation.

The same principle applies in a career conversation. A lab can demonstrate useful understanding without becoming production experience. A contribution to a project can be valuable without turning into a claim that you owned the entire deployment.

Translate a symptom into an actionable next question

When something fails, the first report may be incomplete.

The useful response is to establish what was attempted, what was expected, what happened instead, and which evidence would distinguish the possible causes.

That structure helps technical teams coordinate without turning the conversation into blame or guesswork.

In an interview, it also gives you a better example than “I am good at troubleshooting.” Explain how you narrowed a problem and made the next action clear, while keeping your actual role and uncertainty visible.

Make the explanation fit the listener

A technical peer may need the protocol boundary or the specific evidence. A business stakeholder may need the impact, available options, and next decision.

You should not change the facts between those conversations. You should change the level of detail and the order in which you present them.

That is a practical communication skill. Use plain-language explanations of access risk when the audience does not need a textbook definition. The objective is accurate understanding, not making the topic sound more complex than it is.

End the interaction with a clear next action and owner

A useful technical conversation should leave the work in a clearer state.

Record:

  • Verified result: What is actually known now?
  • Open question: What remains uncertain or outside scope?
  • Next action: A test, clarification, decision, or escalation.
  • Owner: Who is responsible for taking that action?

The next step may be accepting a documented limitation rather than changing another setting. It may also require the team that owns a different component.

Keep a usable record so the clarity survives after the call ends.

Apply the same discipline to a portfolio: show what was verified, what remains outside the claim, and what further work would require. A polished demo without those boundaries tells only part of the story and gives a reviewer less reason to trust the conclusion.

Show these skills through evidence, not personality claims

Instead of writing “excellent communicator” on a resume and stopping there, use an example that shows how you explained a technical issue or coordinated a result.

Keep the example accurate and safe to share. Do not invent customer outcomes or disclose private environments to make the story more impressive.

Technical work and communication reinforce each other when both are grounded in the same evidence.
