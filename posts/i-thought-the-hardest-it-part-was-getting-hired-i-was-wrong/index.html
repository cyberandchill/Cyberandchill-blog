I Thought the Hardest IT Part Was Getting Hired. I Was Wrong.

Getting the first IT job and choosing the right next job are different challenges.

An early role gives you access to real work. Later, you need to decide which responsibilities will develop the career you want rather than simply accepting more work you're capable of doing.

After entry, become more selective about what the next opportunity adds. Looking back at my path through support, networking, security software, and identity, that is a decision I'd examine more closely—not just whether someone was willing to hire me.

Use different criteria for your first job and your next one

An early opportunity may be valuable because it introduces real systems, colleagues, and professional expectations. Once you have that foundation, ask more specific questions:

  • New responsibility: What will I own that I don't already handle?
  • Support: Who will review my work or help with unfamiliar decisions?
  • Future relevance: Which following role would this experience prepare me for?
  • Fit: Do the work and conditions match the direction I now want?

A job can be a sensible first step and a poor fifth step. That doesn't make the original decision wrong; your starting point has changed.

Use the additional experience to improve your criteria. Otherwise, being increasingly employable can lead to a series of acceptable offers that never develop the responsibility you actually hoped to gain.

More responsibility is not always the right responsibility

You may be offered a broader queue, more sites, or an informal leadership role because you are dependable.

Those opportunities can be useful. They can also pull you away from the specialty you want to develop.

Before accepting, ask what the added work will make you better at and whether that capability fits your goals. Do not assume every increase in workload is career progression.

A clear target helps you distinguish a genuine development opportunity from becoming the person who absorbs whatever nobody else owns.

A stable job can make vague ambition easy to postpone

When the immediate pressure of getting hired is gone, it becomes easier to study without deciding where the study leads.

You can spend months collecting interesting knowledge while your application story remains unchanged.

That is why I prefer a concrete target role over a general promise to keep improving. The role gives you a reason to choose one skill and leave another for later.

Why trying to learn every interesting technology can weaken your focus explores that tradeoff without suggesting curiosity is a problem.

Use evidence to choose the next move

Compare your current responsibilities with the work in a role you would actually accept.

Identify which parts you can already support with examples and which parts remain unproven. Then decide whether the gap is best addressed through an internal assignment, a lab, structured study, or a different role.

You do not need a complete lifetime plan. You need a defensible reason for the next significant investment of your time.

The plan should also respect your preferred schedule, location, and working environment. A technically interesting job can still be the wrong life decision.

Review the direction before another year passes

Set a periodic review that asks what new work you can explain and what options have become more realistic.

If the answer is unclear, do not automatically respond with another exam. Revisit the target and the opportunities available around you.

Sometimes the right decision is to stay and build. Sometimes it is to apply. Sometimes it is to change the specialty you are investigating.

The important part is making the decision from evidence rather than assuming movement will happen because you remain busy.
