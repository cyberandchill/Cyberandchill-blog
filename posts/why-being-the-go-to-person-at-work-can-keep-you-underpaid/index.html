Why Being the Go-To Person at Work Can Keep You Underpaid

Being everyone's go-to IT person doesn't automatically mean your career is advancing.

Find out why people depend on you: deep technical judgment, undocumented local knowledge, or constant availability. Each creates a different kind of value—and a different next step.

You don't need to become less helpful. You need to make your contribution understandable, transferable, and connected to the responsibilities you want instead of measuring progress by how often you're interrupted.

Sort the reasons people ask you for help

Look at the requests you repeatedly receive.

They rely on you because… What to examine
You diagnose difficult problems Which technical capability does that demonstrate?
You remember undocumented procedures Can you turn that knowledge into a process others can follow?
You always respond immediately Is availability expanding your responsibility or only your workload?

A single person may fit all three rows. The point is to distinguish them before deciding that more interruptions mean more career growth.

Write down a few recent examples and the judgment each required. Deep troubleshooting can support a specialization story. Local knowledge may need better documentation to become understandable outside the company. Responsiveness is useful, but it doesn't by itself prove you're developing the technical scope of your next role.

Separate repeated rescue work from lasting improvement

If you solve the same access problem every week, investigate whether the process or documentation can be improved through an approved change.

A reusable troubleshooting guide, clearer request requirements, or a verified correction may reduce the need for repeated rescue.

Don't make unauthorized changes to prove initiative. Propose the improvement, identify the owner, and explain how you would evaluate the result.

The aim is to show that you can improve the work, not merely absorb an unlimited amount of it.

Document enough for another person to continue

A useful procedure identifies the situation, the required evidence, the approved steps, and the escalation boundary.

It should help a colleague handle an appropriate case without depending on your memory. Keep security-sensitive details within authorized systems and access controls.

Sharing knowledge does not erase your capability. It can reveal a different kind of contribution: making the team more reliable.

It also gives you a clearer example to discuss than “everyone relies on me,” which is difficult for an outside employer to evaluate.

Discuss responsibility and development explicitly

Bring examples to your manager: recurring problems you handle, improvements you've proposed or completed, and the next responsibility you want to develop.

Ask what formal scope, support, or progression is possible. Don't assume that being appreciated will automatically lead to a role change or compensation adjustment.

The organization may have constraints you cannot solve through extra effort alone.

A clear conversation helps you decide whether to pursue an internal path, build a missing skill independently, or explore external roles.

Build evidence that travels beyond the current environment

You cannot take confidential records or internal procedures into a public portfolio. You can explain your contribution in appropriately generalized terms and build a fictional lab that demonstrates the underlying skill.

For example, repeated access issues might lead you to model a clearer group-based access process in a test environment.

Use the skill hidden behind your current title to identify which part of the work another employer could recognize and use.

The goal is portable capability, not a public archive of your employer's systems.

Protect time for the next move

Being helpful should not consume every hour you intended to use for development or a targeted job search.

Work within your role's expectations and discuss priorities when demands conflict. Outside that work, keep one manageable learning objective connected to your next responsibility.

You may choose to stay because the job fits your life. You may choose to move. Either decision is stronger when it is based on options rather than dependency.

Be useful, but make the usefulness understandable. A career needs more than being the person nobody wants to lose from the current queue.
