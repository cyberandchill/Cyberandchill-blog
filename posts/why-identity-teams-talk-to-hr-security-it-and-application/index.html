Why Identity Teams Talk to HR, Security, IT, and Application Owners

Identity teams need decisions from other teams because access follows work and responsibility, not just account settings.

A role change may originate in HR, require a business owner's approval, involve security requirements, and depend on IT or application teams to implement and verify it.

The ownership varies by organization. Make it explicit in your explanation instead of assuming an identity administrator can decide every stage. A fictional employee transfer is a useful way to map the handoffs.

Start with a fictional employee transfer

Imagine Taylor moving from Customer Operations to Finance Reporting.

The organization needs to establish which old access is no longer appropriate, which new access is approved, and when the change should take effect.

No single console can answer every business question automatically.

The technical process depends on reliable information and accountable decisions. That is why an identity workflow is often a coordination problem as well as an implementation problem.

Identify the trusted source of the employee change

Before changing access, the process needs to know that the transfer is real and when it should take effect.

For your fictional scenario, define:

  • Source: Which designated process supplies the change?
  • Information: Which identity and responsibility are affected?
  • Timing: When should the new state apply?
  • Handoff: How does the event reach the access-decision process?

Many designs use HR information, but not every organization uses the same system or assigns ownership of every attribute to the same team.

Label the source you chose as part of the scenario rather than a universal rule.

This prevents the exercise from beginning with an unexplained administrator deciding that someone has a different job. It connects the technical change to an authoritative business event and makes the next approval and implementation steps understandable.

Resource owners decide the business need for access

The new team or application owner should help determine the permissions needed for the new responsibility.

The old owner may also need to clarify whether any previous access should remain temporarily or be removed immediately under the scenario's rules.

Record the decisions and their reasons. The person who can technically add a group membership may not be the person authorized to approve the business need. Keeping that distinction clear prevents administrative ability from becoming the default approval mechanism.

Security helps define relevant control requirements

The process may need to account for least privilege, sensitive permissions, conflicting responsibilities, or other organizational controls.

The identity team should understand which requirements apply and when to involve the appropriate security or risk owner.

For a learning project, state the control assumptions instead of inventing a universal policy. A fictional example can demonstrate the reasoning without claiming that it satisfies a real organization's compliance obligations.

The useful question is how the access decision remains consistent with the requirements governing the resource.

IT and identity teams implement and verify the approved state

The technical owners map the approved decision to the relevant accounts, groups, roles, or application configuration.

Then they verify the result within their responsibility and coordinate any downstream checks with other owners.

A successful update in one system may not establish the entire user's access outcome. Make those boundaries visible.

Use the handoff between application and identity owners to show how evidence moves with the work instead of leaving each team to repeat the investigation independently.

Document the workflow as a set of decisions and handoffs

For the fictional transfer, create a short record showing the event, approvers, intended access state, implementers, tests, and unresolved exceptions.

Identify who acts when information is missing or the result differs from the expectation.

This can be a useful portfolio artifact even before sophisticated automation is added. It shows that you understand why a lifecycle process needs more than a script that creates or disables accounts.
