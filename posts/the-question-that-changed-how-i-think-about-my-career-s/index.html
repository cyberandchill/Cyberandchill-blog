The Question That Changed How I Think About My Career's Market Value

The career question I find more useful than “Which certification next?” is “What would another employer trust me to take responsibility for?”

It changes the focus from collecting qualifications to explaining work I can do, demonstrate, and support outside my current environment.

My path through network support, security software, and identity made that distinction increasingly useful. It helps identify whether the next investment should build capability, improve evidence, or clarify how existing experience applies elsewhere.

Separate being familiar with a workplace from being useful elsewhere

You can become very effective in one organization because you know its people, history, and unwritten processes.

That knowledge matters. But a new employer doesn't automatically understand its value, and some of it may not transfer directly.

The useful exercise is to identify the capability underneath it. Did you learn to diagnose dependencies, translate requirements, manage access changes, or explain technical problems to customers?

Those are responsibilities you can describe beyond one workplace. The context supports the story, but it shouldn't be the only thing making the story understandable.

Translate a task into the problem it solved

“Worked with user accounts” is a task category. It doesn't tell someone what you were trusted to handle.

A more useful explanation identifies the situation, your role, and the result: verifying an approved request, implementing a bounded change, checking the user's access, or investigating why the expected behavior failed.

I'm not suggesting adding a grand business claim to every routine ticket. A small, accurate example is better than an inflated one.

The point is to make the responsibility visible enough that another person can judge it.

Ask where your evidence is strongest

Some claims are supported by repeated professional work. Others are supported by a single lab, a course, or general familiarity.

Keep those categories separate. They tell you where you can make a strong application today and where you need more practice.

This is also why I don't think a career plan should begin with a long list of technologies. The list can grow indefinitely while the evidence behind each item stays shallow.

Choose one responsibility where better evidence would make a meaningful difference to the roles you can pursue.

Look for work that increases your next set of options

A new task or project can be valuable even before it changes your compensation.

Ask whether it gives you deeper understanding, a responsibility you can explain, or experience with a problem relevant to your intended direction. Also ask what support and authorization you need to do it properly.

Not every extra duty is a development opportunity. Some simply add volume without giving you more capability or decision-making experience.

The tradeoffs behind accepting the wrong promotion are one example of why more responsibility on paper isn't always the same as better career positioning.

Use hiring feedback to test the plan, not judge yourself

Job descriptions and interview conversations can reveal how employers describe the work. They don't define your value as a person or guarantee your compensation.

Use the feedback to check:

  • Level: Are you targeting responsibility your evidence can support?
  • Relevance: Do your examples answer the role's actual questions?
  • Capability: Is a project or deeper practice needed?
  • Explanation: Is useful experience being hidden by vague wording?

Don't interpret every rejection as a requirement for another exam. Several explanations may fit, and one response rarely proves which is correct.

Look for patterns and change the relevant part of the plan. Evidence may support a different role, better presentation, or more focused learning.

That makes the market a source of information for the next decision rather than a verdict that sends you back to studying indefinitely.

Make the next learning decision answer the question

Before starting another course, I would write the responsibility it is meant to support and the evidence I expect to produce afterward.

That keeps the work connected to a career move instead of becoming an endless collection of interesting subjects.
