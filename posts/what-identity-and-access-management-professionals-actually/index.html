What Identity and Access Management Professionals Actually Do All Day

An IAM job may center on support, technical projects, or decisions about who should retain access. Those responsibilities can produce very different workdays.

Ask which kind of work fills the team's calendar before deciding the role suits you. One person may investigate failed sign-ins and build integrations; another may spend most of the week coordinating access reviews.

The categories below help you read beyond the title. They also give you better interview questions than asking whether the company uses a product you've studied.

Operations keeps existing access working

Picture a fictional engineering team whose employees suddenly cannot open their project system. The identity operations person starts by narrowing the problem: which users, which application, which time, and which error?

They might compare an affected account with a working one, inspect the relevant sign-in record, and check whether a recent change explains the difference. They also need to communicate what is known without promising a fix before the cause is understood.

Microsoft documents sign-in logs as a source of identity activity. Reading those records is different from repeatedly resetting a password and hoping.

Operations also includes routine requests. The interesting question is whether you only execute established steps or also diagnose exceptions and improve the process.

Projects change how access works

Now suppose the company purchases a new application. Someone must establish how employees sign in, who receives an account, and who approves access.

The project work might involve gathering requirements, configuring a test integration, coordinating with the application owner, validating access, and planning the production change.

That person needs more than a successful demo. They need a rollback plan, a support handoff, and a clear answer to what happens when the integration fails.

A job focused on projects may offer longer stretches of planned work. It can also involve deadlines, difficult migrations, and meetings with teams that have different priorities. “Project role” does not mean “no pressure.”

Governance work coordinates decisions about existing access

A governance-focused day may involve fewer incidents and more questions about ownership:

  • Contractors: Who approved the access, and is the engagement still active?
  • Transfers: Does someone still need permissions from their former team?
  • Applications: Which owner or manager can review the users and justify access?

Microsoft's governance overview connects identity administration with access processes and oversight. The practical work can include finding the person who can make a defensible decision—not merely clicking a review button.

That may appeal to someone who enjoys organizing ambiguous requests and following decisions through to completion.

If you prefer uninterrupted configuration work, ask how much coordination the job requires. Knowing the tools won't tell you how often you'll be working with managers, application owners, or incomplete access information.

Ask the team for a recent example

Instead of asking whether a job is “technical,” ask what the team completed last week.

A useful answer describes the work, the people involved, and the decisions the role owned. Follow up with: “How much of that would this position handle independently?”

Then ask how time is divided among requests, incidents, projects, and reviews. You are looking for the actual balance, not a polished percentage that nobody can explain.

Test the work before committing to the title

Try one small exercise from the part of IAM that interests you. Diagnose a failed test login, document an application integration, or review a fictional access list.

Notice which activity makes you curious enough to investigate the next question. Also notice which frustrations you can tolerate. Every specialty has them.

A better title is useful. A workday you understand before accepting it is more useful.
