CompTIA A+ Got Me Started. Would I Take the Same Path Again?

Learning A+ material and paying for the A+ exam are separate decisions. Which one makes sense depends on your current knowledge and the role you're pursuing.

Use foundational study to close a real gap, not to repeat somebody else's starting point. I've held foundational certifications, but my later work moved through networking, security software, and identity.

Looking back can help explain that path. It doesn't establish that the same credential is the best next purchase for an experienced IT professional targeting a different responsibility today.

Foundational knowledge can still reveal useful gaps

Someone who has learned mainly on the job may be comfortable with daily tasks but unfamiliar with the surrounding concepts.

A structured set of objectives can expose those gaps. You might discover that you can follow a troubleshooting procedure without explaining the device, operating-system, or networking issue behind it.

Studying a foundational topic is not embarrassing because you have an experienced job title. The purpose is to understand the work more reliably.

The question is whether the whole certification path is necessary or whether selected material addresses the gap more efficiently.

Connect the exam to your current career stage

The same foundational credential can serve different purposes:

Starting point Question to investigate
Seeking a first support role Would the credential strengthen a plausible application or fill important foundations?
Already administering systems Is there a missing foundation, or would targeted review be sufficient?
Targeting identity engineering Does the exam address the responsibility preventing your next move?

Check suitable job descriptions and your own evidence before choosing an answer.

Finish this sentence: “I'm considering this credential because it addresses this requirement or this missing foundation.”

If your only reason is that someone successful took it years ago, you're copying their history. A current decision should account for what you already know and what the next job actually requires.

Keep the fundamentals that identity work depends on

Identity problems can involve endpoints, networks, browsers, directories, and applications. A specialist who ignores those dependencies can struggle to locate a failure.

You do not need a new badge every time you revisit a foundation. You need enough understanding to explain why the dependency matters in the problem you are solving.

For example, a client that cannot reach the appropriate service presents a different investigation from a user who authenticates successfully but lacks application permission.

Learning to separate those cases is more valuable than reciting which certificates belong to which career stage.

Avoid using a basic exam to postpone a harder decision

Sometimes a familiar certification path is attractive because it offers clear lessons, practice questions, and a finish line.

Choosing a specialty is less tidy. It requires comparing jobs, acknowledging gaps, and deciding what work you want to pursue.

Another exam can be useful, but it can also delay that decision. Be honest about which problem you are solving.

Whether an experienced sysadmin should take an entry-level security exam applies the same reasoning to a different credential question.

Choose the next learning step from your current position

Write down your target role, the foundations you already use confidently, and the concepts you cannot yet explain.

Then decide whether the best next step is a course module, an exam, a practical project, or an application. There is no requirement that all useful learning ends in a paid test.

Check the provider's current objectives and requirements before spending money; certification details change, while your decision should remain tied to the role.
