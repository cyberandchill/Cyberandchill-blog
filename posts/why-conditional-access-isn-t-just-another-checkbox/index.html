Why Conditional Access Isn't Just Another Checkbox

A Conditional Access policy isn't proven correct because its switch is on or a user saw an MFA prompt.

Define the intended decision, scope, and safe test before enabling the policy. You need to know which attempts it should affect, which conditions matter, and what evidence will confirm the result.

Treat it as a control to explain and verify, not a checkbox to complete. Keep learning exercises isolated and avoid experiments that could lock out real users.

Write the intended decision before opening the portal

Use a plain-English requirement for an isolated test environment.

For example: “For this fictional test group accessing this test application, require the specified access control under the conditions being evaluated.”

Be explicit about the group, application, conditions, and expected outcome. Avoid a vague objective such as “make the tenant secure.”

Microsoft describes Conditional Access as a policy-based engine that brings signals together to enforce access decisions. Its overview is a useful foundation before attempting a configuration.

Understand the policy's scope and dependencies

A policy can only demonstrate what you actually included in its scope.

Check the targeted identities and resources, the conditions being evaluated, and the relevant controls. Understand prerequisites and licensing for the features you intend to use rather than assuming every tenant supports the same exercise.

Also inspect interactions with other policies. A result may reflect more than the one policy you are looking at.

Your writeup should explain the test environment's relevant configuration, not present one screenshot as proof of a complete security posture.

Plan the safe test before enabling anything

Use an isolated lab or an explicitly authorized test process. Don't experiment broadly in an employer's tenant for a portfolio project.

Read the current platform guidance for deployment and evaluation options, define a limited test population, and establish a recovery approach appropriate to the environment.

Make sure you understand how administrative access will be protected during the exercise. An exclusion or emergency-access arrangement is a deliberate design decision, not a casual shortcut to add after a lockout.

The test should have a known starting state and a way to return to it.

Test an included case and a meaningful comparison case

Build a small matrix before collecting screenshots.

Test detail What to record
Intended user and resource Which access attempt should the policy address?
Triggering condition What should make the relevant control apply?
Comparison case What falls outside this particular requirement?
Result Expected behavior and actual behavior, separately

Seeing a prompt doesn't prove the whole policy behaves correctly. The absence of a fresh prompt also doesn't explain every authentication or session decision.

Inspect the relevant sign-in evidence and policy evaluation for the actual attempt. Keep existing session conditions and the limits of your test visible.

The goal is to support a specific claim about the configured decision—not infer universal protection from one successful demonstration.

Investigate unexpected behavior instead of changing settings randomly

If a result differs from the expectation, check the identity, resource, conditions, and policy interaction before making another change.

Keep track of one modification at a time. Otherwise, you may get the desired result without knowing which change caused it.

The distinction between MFA enrollment and enforcement is especially useful here. Registering an authentication method and applying an access requirement are related but different activities.

Your explanation should show how the evidence led to the next check.

Present the policy as a decision you can explain

A useful portfolio entry states the requirement, scope, relevant dependencies, test matrix, observed results, and recovery considerations.

It should also state that the exercise is a lab and identify any features or scenarios you did not test.

That is more informative than “Configured Conditional Access” because it reveals the reasoning behind the configuration.

The valuable skill is knowing why the policy exists and proving its intended behavior within a clearly defined scope.
