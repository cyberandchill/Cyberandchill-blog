The Uncomfortable Math of Waiting Three More Years to Specialize

Waiting three years to explore a specialty is a decision with potential costs and benefits.

Compare what waiting gives you with what it postpones: relevant experience, hiring feedback, and possible opportunities. That doesn't mean specializing guarantees a raise or that you should leave a stable job immediately.

Use hypothetical comparisons carefully and separate exploration from resignation. You can gather useful information now without committing to an outcome or deadline you cannot guarantee.

Use hypothetical numbers without pretending they are a forecast

Suppose two otherwise comparable career paths differ by $15,000 in annual gross pay for three years. The arithmetic difference is $45,000 before taxes and other factors.

That example is not a prediction about IAM, your current salary, or the result of a certification. It simply shows how an annual difference accumulates.

Real comparisons are more complicated. Benefits, variable pay, working hours, travel, location, stability, and the time required to qualify can change the decision.

Don't turn a hypothetical salary gap into a claim that you're already losing money you were never guaranteed to earn.

Count the experience you would gain on each path

The more important difference may be the work you can demonstrate after three years.

One path might repeat familiar tasks with little change in responsibility. Another might add a relevant specialization, deeper troubleshooting, or experience owning a defined process.

A third might provide stability while you develop skills outside work. That can be a deliberate strategy rather than a failure to advance.

Ask what evidence each path is likely to produce, not only what the title will look like.

Identify a real reason for waiting

There can be good reasons to postpone a job move: family needs, health, financial stability, a worthwhile internal project, or an unusually good working arrangement.

You don't have to defend those priorities with a career-growth slogan.

The question is whether waiting has a purpose and a review point. “I'll stay while I finish this relevant project and reassess afterward” is different from “I'll think about it when things calm down.”

Write the condition that would make you reconsider. Otherwise, the same uncertainty can repeat every year without producing new information.

Explore the option without committing to leave your job

You can learn whether IAM fits while maintaining your current responsibilities.

Start with:

  1. Relevant job descriptions within your real constraints.
  2. An honest comparison with your existing experience.
  3. One small project addressing a meaningful gap.
  4. Conversations about the actual work where appropriate.

Keep employer systems, information, and permissions separate from independent practice. Continue doing the current job professionally.

The low-risk approach to choosing a niche without waiting for permission can help make the exploration concrete.

This lowers the stakes of the first decision. You aren't promising that IAM will replace your job by a particular date; you're gathering evidence about whether the direction is worth pursuing.

That information makes a later choice to stay, move, or change the plan more informed than continuing to wait without testing the assumptions behind the delay.

Set a small deadline for information, not a guaranteed outcome

Choose a review point for something you control.

By then, you might have identified one role family, completed one useful lab, and assessed whether your application materials explain the transition. You might also discover that the specialty doesn't suit you.

Don't make the deadline “earn six figures in 90 days.” That outcome depends on factors beyond a study plan.

A better deadline produces information that lets you make the next decision with less guesswork.

Compare the cost of action with the cost of continued uncertainty

A focused experiment takes time and effort. Doing nothing also uses time, even when it feels less risky.

The practical question is whether a manageable amount of investigation could improve your choices. You don't need to solve your entire career before starting.
