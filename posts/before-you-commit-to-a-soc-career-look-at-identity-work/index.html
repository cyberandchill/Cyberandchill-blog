Before You Commit to a SOC Career, Look at Identity Work

Compare identity work with SOC work before committing to a security specialty. Look at the questions you'd investigate, the daily responsibilities, and the experience you can build on.

Neither field guarantees an easier schedule, higher pay, or less stress. The employer and specific role determine those conditions.

Try a small exercise in each direction, then read actual openings. You'll have a better basis for choosing when the decision reflects work you enjoy and can develop—not simply the image attached to a cybersecurity title.

Compare the questions each role asks

A security operations role may ask you to investigate suspicious activity, determine its significance, and follow the organization's response process.

An identity role may ask why access failed, whether access should exist, or how an application should connect to the organization's identity system.

There is overlap. Identity events can be security signals, and identity teams can participate in incident response. The distinction is the primary responsibility of the particular position.

Read several realistic postings from each area. Highlight the verbs and expected decisions, not just the tool names.

Look for a connection to work you already understand

If you have supported accounts, groups, authentication, or application access, identity work may give you a direct way to build on that experience.

If you enjoy analyzing events, investigating suspicious behavior, and following incident-response procedures, operations work may be appealing.

Neither starting point settles the decision. It gives you a hypothesis to test.

The goal is not to find the specialty that sounds most impressive. It is to find a direction where your existing strengths and willingness to learn create a plausible next move.

Try a small exercise from each direction

For an identity exercise, investigate a fictional user's access failure and document the permission or sign-in path.

For an operations-oriented exercise, work through a legitimate defensive investigation scenario using an authorized training environment and explain the evidence behind your conclusion.

Notice what you enjoy after the initial setup. Do you like the investigation itself, or mainly the appearance of the tools?

Also notice the frustrations you tolerate. Every specialty has incomplete information, repetitive tasks, and work that is less exciting than the marketing suggests.

Ask about schedules and escalation rather than assuming them

For either an IAM or SOC role, ask about:

  • Coverage: Shifts, on-call frequency, and required availability.
  • Changes and incidents: After-hours windows and the responsibilities that trigger escalation.
  • Location and travel: What the employer actually permits or expects.
  • Support: Who helps a new hire investigate unfamiliar problems?

These conditions can matter as much as the technical specialty. Don't assume identity work means quiet business hours or that all SOC teams share one shift pattern.

How cybersecurity workdays differ across roles is a question to investigate directly with the team.

Also compare what felt difficult in your practice exercises: interpreting evidence, understanding an application, clarifying a rule, or communicating uncertainty.

Those observations help you evaluate employment fit. Which dashboard looked more impressive is a much weaker basis for the decision.

Choose a first direction without closing every other door

A focused plan does not require a permanent identity as “an IAM person” or “a SOC person.”

Choose a role to investigate, build relevant evidence, and test your candidacy. Keep the transferable skills: troubleshooting, documentation, communication, and careful handling of access or evidence.

Reconsider the direction when you have specific new information, not whenever another specialty trends online.

The lane comparison and practical projects in The Six-Figure IAM Career Playbook are a low-cost way to evaluate the identity side. The $9 guide does not promise that IAM is the easiest route; it helps you decide whether the work is a sensible route for you.

The best comparison is not between two glamorous titles. It is between two sets of responsibilities you understand well enough to choose deliberately.
