A Good Architecture Diagram Could Save Your IAM Interview

An architecture diagram helps an interview when it makes a system you understand easier to explain.

It can't compensate for missing understanding. A page full of product logos may actually make your answer harder to follow if the relationships and decisions are unclear.

Start with a small map of the scenario: participants, access path, relevant boundaries, and your contribution. Use it to give the interviewer a shared reference before discussing the details.

Draw only what the scenario needs

Choose one project and identify the user, identity system, application, and resource involved.

Include supporting components when they are necessary to explain the behavior. Leave unrelated services out of the main view.

The diagram should answer a question about this scenario, not prove that you know how many tools exist in the ecosystem.

If you need a broader environment diagram, keep it separate from the focused view used for the interview explanation.

Label what components and connections actually do

A product name alone may not tell the listener its role in your project.

Add functional labels such as:

  • Identity provider: Where the relevant identity or authentication function sits.
  • Test application: What the user is trying to access.
  • Directory source: Where the represented identities originate.

Then explain the arrows. Does a line mean authentication, synchronization, an administrative change, or access to a resource?

Don't call every connection “integration.” That can conceal several processes with different failure points and owners.

Your labels should let someone unfamiliar with one product still follow the scenario. The diagram doesn't need to contain every protocol detail. It needs to make the relationships clear enough that you can explain a decision or locate a failure without losing the listener.

Use the diagram to explain the decision you made

Point to the relevant boundary and explain why the design supports the requirement.

For a fictional contractor scenario, you might show where access is assigned and where the application enforces the role. For SSO, you might show the identity provider's responsibility separately from the application's local permission decision.

The diagram should support a clear explanation of your choice and its limitation.

Don't claim that drawing a boundary proves you configured it securely. Link the design to the tests you actually performed.

Locate the failure without losing the listener

When discussing a troubleshooting example, mark the last stage you confirmed worked and the stage where the observed result differed.

This helps avoid a long chronological story in which the listener has to remember every setting you checked.

Explain the evidence, the justified change, and the retest using the same map.

Use a documented login flow when the project involves several authentication participants and the sequence is the main source of confusion.

Give the interviewer room to ask about a boundary before moving on. The diagram should make the discussion easier to navigate, not become a script that prevents useful questions.

Prepare a version safe to share

Use fictional identities and remove secrets, customer information, internal domains, and other details you are not authorized to disclose.

Do not assume a diagram is harmless because it contains no passwords. It may still reveal sensitive architecture or access relationships.

For professional experience, describe an appropriately generalized scenario. For a lab, keep the environment clearly labeled and avoid exposing personal identifiers unnecessarily.

The interview should evaluate your reasoning, not your willingness to share private infrastructure.

Practice explaining the diagram in two passes

First give the short version: the problem, the main systems, and the result.

Then offer the technical path when the interviewer asks for depth. Be ready to explain the assumptions, tests, and parts you did not implement.

If the drawing requires several minutes of decoding before the scenario makes sense, simplify it. If you can't answer questions without reading labels, return to the underlying concept.
